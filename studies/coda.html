What CODA would do to a DeepSeek-V3 layer

Every kernel in one MoE layer: how fast it could run, how fast it actually runs, and which ones a GEMM-epilogue programming model could merge into a neighboring matrix multiply.

CODA (Guo, Zhang, Menon, Guessous, Thakkar, Kim, Dao; May 2026) is a way of writing GPU kernels built around one idea. Most of the work in a Transformer block that isn't a matrix multiply (GEMM) or attention (norms, activations, residual adds, casts) can be done in the epilogue of a neighboring GEMM. The epilogue is the GEMM's last step, when each block ("tile") of its output is still on the chip. Today each of those operations is usually a kernel of its own, which reads the whole activation from HBM (the GPU's main memory) and writes it back. The roofline post priced DeepSeek-V3 as if those other kernels were free. DeepSeek's own profile says they aren't: 2.71 ms of a 9.0 ms forward pass and 5.31 ms of a 14.6 ms backward pass go to "everything else (not a matmul)".03's table counts idle time separately; these are sums of kernel durations. The totals here add up each pass's launch durations, so they differ slightly from 03's wall-clock spans. This page opens that bucket. It takes the 240 kernel launches of one typical layer from the trace and works out what data each one moves. It then estimates the fastest each could run, limited either by the tensor cores (math) or by HBM (memory bandwidth), and asks the CODA question of each: could this be folded into a GEMM's epilogue, and which GEMM?

The short version:

  • The non-GEMM kernels already run at only ×1.6–1.9 the minimum time needed to move their bytes. They can't be made much faster; the only real saving is to stop moving the bytes.
  • Under CODA, a layer's non-GEMM time per microbatch would drop from 8.60 ms to about 2.54 ms. Over a full step that is 2.4 s–2.6 s of a 20.8 s step, at best.
  • DeepSeek's multi-GPU layout causes one problem: part of a norm has to cross the all-to-all. It turns out to cost nothing, because it can ride inside the FP8 scales the all-to-all already sends.
  • The biggest leftover cost is not CODA's to fix: DeepEP's shuffling kernels around the all-to-all, 1.28 ms per layer.
  • Run on the paper's own benchmarks, the same method predicts the measured speedups to within about 0.05.

DeepSeek-V3 is a harder case than the LLaMA block the paper studied, for two reasons. First, the GEMM that follows the MoE's input RMSNorm runs on a different GPU, on the other side of an all-to-all exchange. The paper's trick postpones one piece of the norm into the next GEMM, so here that piece has to travel between GPUs. Second, the MoE's feed-forward layer is a grouped GEMM over many experts, with a routing weight for each token, and the paper's building blocks don't cover that. Both problems turn out to be solvable, and the second half of the page shows how. The first half is just the inventory.

The paper in brief

Every sublayer boundary of a pre-norm block has the same shape: a GEMM, then a residual add, then an RMSNorm, then the next GEMM. Written out, with z the residual stream, γ the norm weight and r the per-row inverse RMS (one number per token):

y = RMSNorm(xW₀ + z, γ) W₁ = ( r · (xW₀ + z) ⊙ γ ) W₁ = r · ( ((xW₀ + z) ⊙ γ) W₁ )

The residual add and the multiply by γ only need the values inside one tile, so they fit in the first GEMM's epilogue. The scale r doesn't: it needs a sum of squares across the whole hidden dimension, which is wider than a tile. So the first GEMM's epilogue writes out a partial sum of squares for each tile, and a small kernel adds the partials up. Because r is a single number per row, it can be pulled through the second GEMM (the last form of the equation) and applied in the second GEMM's epilogue instead of before it. The standalone norm kernel used to read and write the full activation. Now it shrinks to a few bytes of partial sums per tile.

The same trick covers more than RMSNorm. SwiGLU and RoPE work on adjacent pairs of output columns, and on Hopper each pair already sits in one thread's registers. Cross-entropy needs a max and a sum of exponentials, which can be computed per tile and combined afterward. The backward pass works too, with one identity. RMSNorm's backward needs the per-row number s = Σcols ∇h₂ ⊙ h₂, which isn't available until the next GEMM in the backward has run. The paper points out that Σ ∇y ⊙ y, taken one GEMM boundary later, is the same number. So it can be built up as partial sums in an epilogue that already has y and ∇y.

The paper measures kernels and blocks on a single H100 against cuBLAS + torch.compile, Liger and FlashInfer. It says nothing about MoE, multi-GPU setups or FP8 block scaling. That is what this page adds.

One layer, every launch

DeepSeek published a profile of one GPU from a 64-GPU H800 run of their EP64 training setup. (03 describes it: a forward chunk and a backward chunk from different microbatches run interleaved, four MoE layers each, with routing simulated as perfectly balanced.) Averaging the four layers gives 240 kernel launches per layer: 84 in the forward, 129 in the backward for input gradients, 17 for weight gradients, and 10 DeepEP communication kernels on their own stream.

Read top to bottom, the attention column is the attention sub-layer:

  • a fused residual add + RMSNorm;
  • two FP8 casts of its output, one for each of the two down-projections that read it (that fork is the first dot on the spine);
  • the two down-projections; RMSNorms on their two outputs (the latents); FP8 casts of those; the two up-projections;
  • four launches for RoPE and the layout copies around it;
  • FlashAttention;
  • two more copies of its output: an E5M6 copy saved for the backward, and an FP8 copy for the output projection;
  • the output projection.

Its output and the residual then cross to the MoE column (the labeled stubs) and enter the second fused residual add + RMSNorm. Next comes the router: one small GEMM plus 33 launches of PyTorch glue code. Most of those take a few microseconds; two of them are 70 µs sorts. After the dispatch, the MoE has four GEMMs and six real kernels around them: the expansion of each token into one row per expert, two transposes of the local experts' weights, a fused SwiGLU × routing weight → FP8 kernel, the local sum of each token's expert outputs before the combine, and two FP8 casts of the gate/up GEMM outputs.

Those last two casts deserve a note, because drawing the wires corrected them. Judged by their shapes alone, they looked like re-quantizations of the SwiGLU output for the weight gradients. But no launch in the trace reads such a tensor. Meanwhile, the backward's two "dequantize the gate/up stash" kernels read an FP8 gate/up tensor that no forward launch had produced. So these casts are the FP8 copy of the SwiGLU input, saved for the backward, which is exactly DeepSeek's §3.3.3 policy. Their timings fit a 4,096-wide cast running at 1.5× its minimum time. The first draft of this page counted only half their bytes.

Once every launch is priced, three things stand out.

  1. The memory-bound kernels already run fairly close to their limit. The forward's 73 non-GEMM launches take 2.71 ms, against a minimum (the time just to move their bytes through HBM) of 1.40 ms: ×1.9. The backward's run at ×1.6. The big casts, norms and SwiGLU kernels each take 1.3–2× their minimum. There is no big efficiency win hiding inside these kernels. The only way to make them much cheaper is to not move the bytes at all.
  2. The GEMMs are nowhere near memory-bound: in every GEMM node the bronze marker sits far to the left of the pink bar. An epilogue can add a lot of memory traffic before its GEMM gets any slower.
  3. The biggest non-GEMM items aren't the ones CODA targets. Around the all-to-all sit two expansions, two local reductions and two routing bookkeeping kernels: 1.28 ms per layer. By design they move whole routed tensors (32,768 rows × 7,168), and no GEMM sits next to them.

Where the kernels go

DeepSeek-V3's layer has the paper's GEMM → norm → GEMM pattern in three places, plus two more that the paper's block doesn't have. In forward order:

The input norm → the MLA down-projections

The layer starts with the residual add and RMSNorm before the Q and KV down-projections. In the paper, this norm would ride along in the previous sublayer's last GEMM. Here, that GEMM is the expert down-projection, and it runs on some other GPU. What arrives on this GPU is the combine: DeepEP adds up each token's eight expert outputs from across the GPUs and writes the result. So there's no GEMM to host the epilogue, but there is something better. The combine kernel assembles each token's complete 7,168-wide row. That means it can add the residual, compute the exact RMS of the row (no partial sums, no extra kernel), multiply by γ, and write the FP8 copy the down-projections want, with r folded into the FP8 scales (explained below). That isn't a GEMM epilogue. It's the same idea applied to a communication kernel, and it would be a change to DeepEP, not to CODA. It removes the fused norm, both FP8 casts and both scale transposes: 215 µs of launches per layer. In their place come two extra tensor writes inside a kernel whose speed is limited by the network, not by HBM.

The down-projections then take on the latent norms. The Q down-projection's output is 1,536 wide, which is twelve 128-column tiles. So its epilogue writes twelve partial sums of squares per row, and the Q up-projection applies rq in its own epilogue. That is exactly the paper's GEMM → RMSNorm → GEMM. The KV down-projection writes the kv latent's partial sums the same way. Its last 64 columns are krope, so it also applies RoPE to them, pair by pair, in the same epilogue. Everything between the down- and up-projections folds away: two latent norms, two layout copies, two FP8 casts and two scale transposes. What's left is two small reductions that read 12 and 4 partial sums per row.

The up-projections → FlashAttention

The Q up-projection's epilogue applies rq, rotates the 64 RoPE columns of each of the 128 heads (pairwise again; each pair sits side by side in the accumulator), and writes Q in the layout FlashAttention reads. Today that takes a GEMM, a RoPE kernel, two layout copies and another RoPE kernel: 314 µs, of which the copies alone move 400 MB. The KV up-projection likewise applies rkv and writes K and V.

FlashAttention itself is outside CODA's scope, since it is already its own fused kernel. But its output tile is exactly one head wide, which is also exactly one 128-value FP8 scale block. So the two casts after it (the E5M6 copy saved for the backward and the FP8 input to the output projection: 293 µs) could be written straight from FlashAttention's output tile, instead of reading the output back from memory. That would be a FlashAttention epilogue, not a GEMM one. This page counts the two writes as staying and only drops the reads.

The output projection → the MoE input norm → three readers

This is the boundary the paper's identity was designed for, and it's where DeepSeek-V3 departs from the paper. The output projection's epilogue adds the residual, multiplies by γ, writes partial sums of squares, and writes x ⊙ γ. A small reduction then computes r. So far, this is the paper. But the normalized MoE input has three readers, not one:

  • the router GEMM (BF16, on this GPU);
  • the shared expert's gate/up GEMM (FP8, on this GPU);
  • the routed experts' gate/up GEMMs, on up to four other nodes, on the far side of the dispatch all-to-all.

Each has to apply r in its own epilogue. For the router and the shared expert, that's the paper's Kernel 5 and Kernel 6 (r must be applied before the sigmoid and before the SwiGLU, respectively). For the routed experts, r has to travel across the all-to-all along with the token.

It can travel for free. DeepSeek's FP8 recipe quantizes activations in blocks of 1 × 128 values, each block with one fp32 scale, so the dispatch already sends 56 scales per token alongside the 7,168 FP8 values. Multiplying a row by r before quantizing doesn't change its FP8 values at all, because each block's scale absorbs any factor shared by the whole row. Only the 56 scales change, all by the same factor r. So the output projection's epilogue quantizes the tensor without r, the small reduction kernel multiplies each token's 56 scales by its r (a pass over 0.9 MB), and the dispatch sends exactly what it sends today. The expert GEMM on the other side dequantizes with the scales it was going to read anyway; it never knows a norm was postponed. That means zero extra bytes on the network and zero changes to the expert GEMM's main loop. The numerics match the paper's delayed-scale numerics, up to one fp32 rounding per scale.If r is sent separately instead, it costs 4 bytes per dispatched token copy, on top of the 7,392 bytes each copy already costs: 0.05% more dispatch traffic. Cheap either way; folding it into the scales is simply free.

On the far side, the routed gate/up GEMM is where DeepSeek has already fused the most. Their swiglu_forward_with_weight_and_per_token_cast_to_fp8 kernel does SwiGLU, multiplies by the routing weight and quantizes, all in one pass. CODA moves that pass into the GEMM's epilogue: SwiGLU on each adjacent pair of gate and up columns, the routing weight loaded once per row, and the 128-value quantization done within the tile. The arithmetic saved is small. What disappears is the 268 MB bf16 gate/up intermediate, which the GEMM writes and the fused kernel reads back: the GEMM's output shrinks from 268 MB to 67 MB of FP8. The shared expert gets the same benefit at one eighth the size. The expert down-projection's epilogue has nothing to absorb in the forward. What follows it is the local sum over each token's expert copies and the combine, and neither of those works within a tile.

The backward: the statistic rides the combine

In the backward, fusion runs the other way: each operation's backward step attaches to the GEMM that produces the gradient of that operation's output. The expert down-projection's input-gradient GEMM takes on SwiGLU's backward. It reads the saved FP8 gate/up tile directly, instead of a separately dequantized bf16 copy. It writes d(gate/up) in FP8 with both scale layouts: row-wise for its own next GEMM, column-wise for the weight gradient. The routing weight's gradient is a per-row dot product of the SwiGLU output with its gradient, which is a reduction within the tile plus a small extra sum. This one host absorbs seven of today's launches, worth 1.1 ms: the dequantization, the fused forward-backward SwiGLU, the dot product, two re-quantizations, a cast and a copy.

The backward of the MoE input norm is where the all-to-all comes back. Its per-row number is s = Σcols ∇h₂ ⊙ h₂, taken over the normalized input and its total gradient. That total gradient is the sum of three GEMM outputs: the router's, the shared expert's and the routed experts'. The last of these arrives through the backward combine. The paper's identity moves s one GEMM boundary later, to Σ ∇y ⊙ y over each reader's output. That sum splits cleanly across the three readers, so each input-gradient GEMM can write its own partial. For the routed experts, the partial is per routed row and is computed on the GPU that holds the expert. It then has to be summed per token across the backward combine: 4 bytes per routed row, next to the 14,336 bytes of gradient the combine already carries for that row. Whichever of the three input-gradient GEMMs runs last applies the norm backward (the figure picks the shared expert's), adding in the other two gradients by loading their tiles. That epilogue replaces eight launches: the recompute of the norm, the standalone norm backward, the adds that accumulate the gradients, and the FP8 casts of the gradient that follows.

The MLA backward is the paper's chain with nothing distributed about it. FlashAttention's own dQ postprocess kernel takes on RoPE's backward and writes dQ in FP8 in both layouts. The Q up-projection's input-gradient GEMM applies rq, takes on the q-latent norm's backward, and writes the partial sums of s for the input norm one boundary earlier. The KV side mirrors it, with one real reduction left over: summing dkrope over the 128 heads it was copied to. The Q down-projection's input-gradient GEMM adds in the KV down-projection's result by loading its tile, applies the input norm's backward, and adds the residual gradient. Nineteen of the attention backward's launches recompute the up-projections and RoPE, because of DeepSeek's checkpointing policy. The recomputed GEMMs stay, and everything around them folds into their epilogues, as in the forward.

What stays

A fair accounting needs the list of what CODA doesn't touch. Largest first, per layer:

  • The four expansion and reduction kernels around the all-to-all, plus the two routing bookkeeping kernels: 1.28 ms, with a minimum of 850 µs. Their shape comes from DeepEP. If they go anywhere, it's into DeepEP's dispatch and combine, which already read those rows.
  • FlashAttention's backward preprocess and dQ conversion (476 µs), which are FlashAttention's own epilogues.
  • The router's 58 small launches across forward and backward (333 µs). Their HBM minimum is a few microseconds; what they mostly pay for is launch overhead. The fix is one hand-written routing kernel, which CODA's building blocks can't express (a group-limited top-k over 256 columns is two tiles wide).
  • The E5M6 round trip in the backward. No GEMM produces the attention output there, so one pass from E5M6 to transposed FP8 remains (today it takes three).
  • The sum of dkrope over heads.
  • The DeepEP kernels themselves, which 03 prices on the network.

Pricing the launches also turned up two things that have nothing to do with CODA. First, DeepSeek's FP8 GEMM transposes its weight on every call: nine weight transposes per layer per microbatch, 329 µs, on weights that don't change during the step. Second, in four places the same activation is quantized twice for two readers (the normalized input for the two down-projections, forward and backward), and the MoE input and the gate/up stash are dequantized to bf16 only to be re-quantized in the other scale layout (322 µs). None of this needs a new programming model. It needs the kernel that produces a tensor to know who will read it. Put another way, that is CODA's whole argument.

HBM: bandwidth now, bytes later

Separately, we check whether the extra memory traffic the epilogues add slows down their host GEMMs. Measured in bytes, in every case but one it stays well under the GEMM's tensor-core time. (An epilogue's other cost, the instructions it runs, isn't measured in bytes; that comes two sections down.) The exception is the Q down-projection's input-gradient GEMM in the backward. It has only 62 µs of tensor-core work (K = 1,536), and it would host the input norm's backward, an epilogue that reads three 59 MB tensors. Its HBM time becomes 75 µs, 13 µs longer than its math. That's still a fraction of the 170 µs of standalone kernels it replaces. It just isn't free: a small GEMM doesn't leave much time to hide extra work behind.

Per layer and microbatch, that's 8.60 ms of non-GEMM kernel time today and 2.54 ms projected, next to 23.8 ms of GEMMs and attention that don't change. In DeepSeek's step, each GPU runs 7.5 such microbatches through 58 MoE layers. So the saving is 2.6 s of kernel time on a 20.8 s step, about 13%. That's an upper bound. It drops to 2.5 s if the SwiGLU epilogues run serialized at precise-math rates (below).An upper bound because 03's trace shows compute and communication neck and neck: the communication stream is busy about as long as the compute stream. Cutting 6 ms of compute per layer exposes communication time unless the all-to-all also gets faster, and the all-to-all's own packing kernels are the largest thing CODA leaves behind. In bytes, the forward's non-GEMM launches move 4.7 GB through HBM per layer and microbatch, and would move 1.8 GB. The backward goes from 11.4 GB to 3.1 GB.

HBM capacity is a smaller story, and it's mostly about short-lived tensors. CODA doesn't change what the backward needs saved: the paper's SwiGLU backward reads the saved (interleaved) pre-activation, which is exactly the FP8 gate/up stash DeepSeek already keeps. So the memory post's accounting of saved activations still holds. What changes is what sits in memory between kernels. Today the routed gate/up GEMM writes a 268 MB bf16 intermediate that lives until the SwiGLU kernel consumes it. The attention output exists in bf16, E5M6 and FP8 at the same time. And the backward dequantizes a 134 MB stash into a 268 MB bf16 copy before using it. Under CODA, those intermediates only ever exist as tiles on the chip. On the other side of the ledger, the saved activations grow where the forward now writes things the backward used to recompute or re-quantize: the normalized input and the MoE input in transposed FP8 layout (+29 MB each per layer). In exchange, the bf16 latents (−16 MB) and three recompute kernels go away. The net is a few tens of MB per layer and microbatch, in one direction or the other. CODA pays off in bandwidth, not capacity.

Who runs the epilogue

The test above only counts bytes: an epilogue is hidden if the memory traffic it adds fits under its host GEMM's tensor-core time. That's the right test for memory bandwidth. It says nothing about the other thing an epilogue costs, which is instructions.

Inside each thread block (CTA), a GEMM is a pipeline. The tensor cores finish an output tile. A few warps then load the accumulator, do the epilogue math, stage the result through shared memory and store it, while the tensor cores start on the next tile. With two accumulator buffers, each tile takes max(main loop, epilogue) in steady state. So the epilogue is free only as long as it's the shorter of the two, and only a few warps run it. An instrumented trace of a warp-specialized BF16 GEMM on a GB300 (256 × 256 tiles, four epilogue warps per SM; the field guide in the sources) shows how quickly that stops being true:

  • A plain convert-and-store epilogue (about 2.5 µs per tile) already takes 2.3× as long as the main loop at K = 256.
  • SiLU with fast-math exp adds little: 3.4 µs per tile, 0.7× the main loop at K = 1,024, so it stays hidden.
  • SiLU with precise exp takes 12 µs per tile, 3.2× the main loop, and the tensor cores wait on the epilogue for two thirds of the kernel. The fused kernel took 174 µs, against 131 µs for the plain GEMM plus a separate SiLU kernel. That's despite the separate kernel paying for a 256 MiB round trip through HBM for its intermediate (with 0.17% L2 hits on it, against a 126 MiB L2).

The separate kernel won because it ran 52 warps per SM, while the epilogue had four. The same SiLU math forced onto four persistent warps per SM ran five times slower, with no warp ready to issue on 86% of scheduler cycles. Fusion saves bytes but spends instruction-issue slots, and the two are measured on different meters.

Three lessons carry over to DeepSeek's layer.

First, the budget an epilogue has to fit under grows with K. At a 128 × 128 tile, the H800's main loop at the measured FP8 peak takes 3.0 ns per unit of K: 21 µs per tile at K = 7,168. That is the K of the MLA down-projections, both gate/up GEMMs and both FC2 input gradients; the output projection (16,384) and the up-projection input gradients (24,576 and 32,768) have more. At the field guide's rates, a 128 × 128 epilogue costs 0.6 µs plain, 0.9 µs with fast-math SiLU, and 3.0 µs with precise SiLU. Seven times the field guide's K is the difference between an epilogue that sets the kernel's pace and one that hides under it. If epilogue and main loop overlap, no host on this page is exposed at any of the three rates. The thin hosts are the up-projections (K = 512 for KV, 1,536 for Q) and the Q-down input gradient (1,536), at 1.5 µs and 4.6 µs per tile. What they host is simple per-element work (a per-row scale, a rotation, tile loads), so they fit too, with a third of the margin.

Second, the schedule, and here the H800 is the harder machine. The GB300 kernel can overlap because Blackwell keeps its accumulators in tensor memory and hands them to separate epilogue warps. Hopper keeps them in the registers of the warpgroups that issue the WGMMAs, and those same warps run the epilogue. The next tile's main loop can overlap the epilogue only in a "ping-pong" kernel, where two consumer warpgroups take turns on tiles. DeepGEMM's FP8 kernel is cooperative, not ping-pong: both math warpgroups work on one tile and store it before starting the next. Their CUDA cores are also already busy during the main loop, promoting the 128-block FP8 partial sums with FFMAs. So on the H800 each tile costs main loop plus epilogue, and every instruction added to the epilogue is added to the GEMM.

Under that model, the simple per-element work costs nothing beyond what the hosts already do (load, convert and store the tile; the residual and stash tiles are the bytes already counted above). The transcendental work costs the difference between a SiLU epilogue and a plain one. That work is SwiGLU forward in the two gate/up GEMMs, SwiGLU backward in the two FC2 input gradients, and the sigmoid in the router, and it adds 24 µs per layer at fast-math rates, 253 µs at precise rates. The standalone kernels those epilogues replace (DeepSeek's fused SwiGLU × weight → FP8, its fused forward-and-backward, and the shared expert's three) take 554 µs today. Fast-math SwiGLU in the epilogue is a clear win. Precise SwiGLU still wins, but gives back nearly half of what it saves. The trace doesn't say which exp DeepSeek's standalone kernel uses. The field guide's table is a warning that the choice is worth 4× in epilogue time, and at a smaller K it flips the result.

Third, registers, which the field guide calls the real limit on independent work in an epilogue. A 128 × 128 fp32 accumulator spread over the 256 threads of two warpgroups is 64 registers per thread, and DeepGEMM holds two of them (the WGMMA accumulator and the promoted sum). The SwiGLU epilogue needs each pair's gate and up values in registers at once. The 128-block quantization needs the largest absolute value across the quad, plus a second reduction for the column layout. The two scale layouts each need their own staging. None of this is modeled here. The field guide's four warps got most of their throughput back by batching 128 elements per thread at 190 registers. A Hopper epilogue has no such room, which argues for reading the precise-rate number as the H800 estimate and the fast-rate number as Blackwell's.

Calibration: the paper's own benchmarks

Everything above is prediction. The paper, though, measured. On a single H100 in BF16, it timed every kernel in its suite against cuBLAS + torch.compile at hidden sizes 2,048, 4,096 and 8,192. It did so at kernel level, with the raw GEMM as a ceiling, and at block level, for each sublayer pattern and a full layer. Those measurements let us test this page's method on cases where the answer is known.

So the method was run on the paper's benchmarks exactly as on DeepSeek's trace. Each kernel is priced at max(FLOPs ÷ measured BF16 peak, bytes ÷ HBM bandwidth), counting each operand once. The baseline is the compiled reference the paper's code runs: cuBLAS for the matmul and one fused Triton kernel for what follows, so the GEMM output is written once and read back once. CODA is the paper's Kernels 4–10, plus the small reductions over partial sums. In the layer backward it also includes the standalone RoPE-backward kernel the paper's code keeps. The predicted speedup is the ratio of the two totals. The measured bars are digitized from the paper's vector figures (bar tops accurate to about ±0.003; the ± shown is the paper's own error bar over 30 runs). Nothing is fitted.

At block level, which is the level this page uses for DeepSeek, prediction and measurement agree to about the width of the error bars. Across the 21 block-and-size combinations, the predicted speedup is within 0.05 of the better of the paper's two implementations, and within 0.02 for half of them. The trend is the one the model forces. Fusion saves bytes; bytes grow with d while FLOPs grow with d², so the speedup shrinks as 1/d, from 1.22 at d = 2,048 to 1.02–1.06 at 8,192, just as the bars do. The layer backward is the one place the model needs a fact that isn't in the paper's figure: CODA's code leaves RoPE's backward as a separate kernel. Counting it, the predicted layer-backward speedup at d = 2,048 is 1.07, against a measured 1.02–1.06. With that kernel fused too, it would be 1.13.

At kernel level, the paper gives the raw GEMM as a ceiling, which splits the error into two parts.

The baseline side is where the method misses. The compiled reference takes 1.0–2.6× its byte minimum. (That's the "× floor" column: the non-GEMM time implied by the measured ceiling, divided by the operand-once minimum.) It's worst for the backward kernels and RoPE, whose references convert to fp32 and make copies, and best for the plain row scale and cross-entropy, whose references are one pass over one tensor. That's the same ×1.3–2 the DeepSeek trace shows for its own kernels. It's also why this page scales DeepSeek's minimums by the measured ratio instead of quoting them directly.

The CODA side is where the method is nearly right, and wrong in a way that teaches something. CODA's kernels come in slower than the raw GEMM by 1%–26% (the "epilogue" column: ceiling ÷ CODA − 1). That cost tracks the serialized sum of the epilogue's extra bytes (the next column), not the zero the max() rule predicts. Epilogues that compute an exp (SwiGLU, cross-entropy) cost a few percent more, at about the field guide's fast-math rate. Both match the Hopper schedule described two sections back: the epilogue's loads, stores and math run after the main loop on the same warps, and are paid in full.

Applied back to DeepSeek's layer, the calibration says two things. First, DeepSeek's minimums are already scaled by measured ratios, so the baseline-side error is already accounted for. Second, the rule for host GEMMs should be a sum, not a max. Adding every host's epilogue bytes in series costs 269 µs per layer, on top of the 24 µs–253 µs of transcendental work, against 6.10 ms saved. The low end of the step saving becomes 2.4 s. The fraction is smaller than on the paper's kernels because DeepGEMM's tiles are FP8 at K = 7,168. That's twice the main-loop time per output byte of the paper's BF16 tiles at K = 4,096, and seven times the field guide's.

What would settle it

Everything said above about DeepSeek's layer is a model: two estimates and a verdict per launch, calibrated on the paper's blocks but never measured on this layer. Each row of the savings is a claim about one pair of kernels, at a shape the trace gives exactly. The field guide's method applies to each one directly: matched implementations, warmed-up CUDA-graph timings, Nsight for warps and issue slots, and a restricted standalone stand-in before a fused kernel exists. The table lists the experiment that would confirm or refute each number, most important first. Only the first can change the headline. What no microbenchmark can settle is the step-level number. 03's trace shows the compute and communication streams busy for about the same time. Taking six milliseconds per layer off the compute stream exposes the all-to-all unless it gets faster too, and the expansion and reduction kernels around it are the largest thing CODA leaves behind. Settling it needs the full EP64 run, or 03's timing model with these kernel times fed back in.

Caveats

  • The trace is one GPU of a 64-GPU profiling run with balanced routing, averaged over four layers. Kernels whose work scales with routed rows are normalized to 32,768 rows, as in 03.
  • The rates are 03's measured GEMM peaks and the data-sheet HBM bandwidth. So "×2.0 above its minimum" is relative to a minimum no real kernel reaches. The meaningful comparison is between kernels on the same meter.
  • CODA's verdicts assume a grouped FP8 GEMM whose epilogue can load per-row vectors (the routing weight, r) and tiles (the residual, the gate/up stash), do reductions within a tile, and write FP8 with 128-block scales in both layouts. The paper's prototype is a dense BF16 CuTeDSL GEMM on Hopper. So this is the paper's building blocks applied to DeepGEMM's shape, not something the paper measured.
  • The all-to-all plumbing (r in the scales in the forward, ŝ partials on the backward combine, the residual and norm in the combine) is a DeepEP change, argued here only at the level of bytes.
  • The epilogue execution rates come from one GB300 trace (four epilogue warps on a 256 × 256 BF16 tile). They are carried over to the H800 per output element per SM, with no correction for clock speed or warp count. Some differences suggest they're pessimistic for Hopper: it has eight consumer warps, and its accumulators are already in registers (no TMEM load). Others suggest they're optimistic: the serialized epilogue and the already-spent register budget. And SwiGLU computes one exp per column pair, not per element, which halves the transcendental count the SiLU rate assumes.
  • Nothing here models overlap. The figure prices kernels; 03 is where the kernels meet the network.
