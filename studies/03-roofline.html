Working the roofline for DeepSeek-V3 on Hopper

In part two, we worked through how memory constraints force us into certain parallelism choices simply to get the model to fit into memory. However, I cheated slightly in the memory exercise: for example, we simply assumed that full FSDP (ZeRO-3) wouldn't be a good idea for DeepSeek-V3 on Hopper. How did we know this? A roofline analysis for FLOPs and distributed communications can easily tell us that we would be communication bound. So in this part, we are going to work out the DeepSeek-V3 roofline in more detail than you ever wanted to know. (A future promise: we could also consider a harder problem, where we the infra team are codesigning a model architecture with the research team. Why does DSv3 make the choices it does? That will be another post!)

In particular, one pedagogical objective in this essay is to explain the underlying reasoning behind the common approximations that are used when we do a rooflining analysis. The 6ND compute approximation is really useful because it is easy to keep in your head and cuts out the complication about having to reason in detail about what the inside of your model looks like. But at the same time, if you haven't thought about why this approximation is valid, it can seem like magic, and I want to explain in enough detail so that you could derive this formula from first principles, and you can also intuitively understand when you shouldn't use this approximation.

The overall plan

Unlike HBM rooflines, where the memory transfer you have to do into each GEMM is usually inviolable,The reason you can't "fuse" two GEMMs together, or even usefully fuse into a GEMM prologue, is because input tiles are used multiple times when computing the GEMM: to avoid repeatedly recomputing an input tile, you need to store it somewhere that all of your SMs can access, and has enough space to store everything you need. Usually HBM is the most convenient thing that actually fits. we have a large degree of control over what distributed communication we do, because we have to pick our parallelism. However, as we saw in the previous post, we desire to make our model bigger and we wish to do strong scaling over a bigger GPU cluster, and these lead us to want to parallelize. But parallelization means distributed communication, and distributed communication can then become a bottleneck. This is where the roofline analysis comes into play.

So a way to think about what we're trying to do is we're trying to solve a problem (our model doesn't fit; we are trying to make good use of our GPUs). We will solve our problem by adding parallelization. But these parallelization introduce communication costs. However, thanks to overlapping, it may be possible that we don't need to pay for these costs (it's free because it's perfectly overlapped). But we need to assess if it is actually possible to hide these costs. Thus we do roofline to cheaply see what we expect to happen.

So to do the roofline, we have to think about what exact parallelization/distributed communication we are considering to assess the impact. From our DSv3 memory analysis, there are already the parallelisms we were forced into in order to fit it in memory:

  1. Expert parallelism
  2. ZeRO/FSDP (1 or 3?)
  3. Pipeline parallelism

Tensor and context parallelism don't appear: DeepSeek-V3 trains with TP1, and at a 4K sequence length there's no need to split sequences.

We use the roofline to see how much compute we have to cover up these communications, and then we can compare to see if we are likely to be comms bound.

How many FLOPs do we need to do

How many FLOPs does it take to train a model? In part 1, we mentioned in passing that we often approximate this by considering only matmuls, as they usually end up taking the bulk of the FLOPs in this model: the classic training flops approximation is 6ND (where N is the number of activated parameters in the model, D is the total number of training tokens, and the 6 FLOPs per parameter per token derives from the multiply and add you need per parameter per token in forwards, and the fact that backwards costs roughly twice the forward pass). If we are reasoning about the wall time of a single training step, we can set D to the number of tokens in a global batch (global batch size x sequence length). Importantly, the global batch size is typically set by science (infra cannot freely adjust it the way they can adjust parallelism), because at a critical batch size, increasing batch size yields diminishing returns on optimization efficiency.

6ND is a really handy approximation since it is so simple (you don't need to know anything about the inside of the model! Just count up the parameters!) However, I want to more carefully explain why this approximation makes sense. When we say that non-matmul compute is negligible, we are making an argument about asymptotics while we scale compute. This might seem like a funny thing to do for DeepSeek-V3: you can have any size you like, as long as it's 671B. What exactly are you scaling?

In pretraining research, it is natural to think of an architecture as actually a family of architectures of different sizes, designed to be as similar to each other as possible, so that we can do science on the smaller sizes, fit scaling laws, and then extrapolate them to the expensive hero run. What happens to the FLOPs of a model as we increase compute, trying to keep the architecture otherwise the same? Given some compute budget C, here is a standard recipe for defining this family (for example, this matches this scaling ladder design in the MAI-Thinking tech report):

  • Per Chinchilla, we will choose to keep tokens-per-parameter (TPP) fixed, so that our parameter count and number of tokens grow proportionally.
  • We will choose to keep the aspect ratio (AR) of our model fixed, so that the depth and width of our model are proportionally related as we increase parameter count.
  • We will choose to keep our compute allocation between attention and MLP fixed, so the hidden size and intermediate size (the MLP hidden size) are proportionally related as we increase the width of our network.
  • We will choose to keep the MoE sparsity structure fixed: so the number of experts and how many experts we route to stay fixed even as compute grows.

When we increase depth, all of the transformer block FLOPs increase uniformly linearly with depth. However, when we increase width, while non-matmul compute increases linearly, the learned linear transformations (matmuls that are activation x weight) increase quadratically, since both the input and output spaces get bigger. This means that, in the limit, learned linear transformation FLOPs dominate everything else.

There is a caveat: inside attention, there are attention activation matmuls that don't (directly) take a weight matrix. These don't scale in parameter count, but instead scale on sequence length. It's common to pretrain with some fixed sequence length, and then only scale the sequence length in some midtraining for context length. This commonly results in a situation where the 6ND approximation is good for pretraining (sequence length not too long) but bad for midtraining (long sequence length means attention is now a bottleneck). We'll delay addressing this until we move on from rooflines to try to build a more accurate model.

Expert parallelism

How much time do the dispatch and combine communications take in the expert parallel region? This is a bit more annoying to do in the Hopper era, because our EP spans across nodes, rather than being in a single NVL72 domain as would be the case in GB200/GB300. But it is not too complicated, and we know directionally what the roofline should look like, as the DeepSeek-V3 technical report specifies that they have roughly a 1:1 computation-to-communication ratio (e.g., as seen in their published profile traces) due to the cross-node EP communication. Let's assemble our assumptions for our roofline:

  • We'll assume uniform routing: the nodes a token is routed to are a uniform pick among the nodes of the EP group (including our own).
  • We will assume use of deduplication. The idea is that if multiple GPUs on the same node require the same token, we don't have to broadcast it multiple times on IB; instead, we can dispatch it once and then use NVLink to locally distribute it to the correct GPU. Additionally, DSv3 has a routing constraint that a maximum of four nodes can be routed to, so we're guaranteed to get at least a 2x bandwidth reduction from deduplication.
  • Infiniband is full-duplex, meaning that we can simultaneously transmit data at the link rate in both directions simultaneously. Thus we will analyze the overall communication needed simply by looking at how much data any given node gets on the receive side.
  • We will assume that the local NVLink is fast enough relative to Infiniband that we can neglect its bandwidth for the purpose of this calculation. It's only about 3.2x faster on H800 (160 GB/s vs 50 GB/s), but the technical report notes that this lets a token reach up to 3.2 experts per node before NVLink binds, and eight experts over four nodes is only two per node.

Let's work out dispatch by hand.

  1. Hidden size is 7168.
  2. Routed MoE layers are 61 − 3 = 58.
  3. EP64 with eight GPUs per node means the EP group spans eight nodes.
  4. Every token goes to eight experts, but node-limited routing puts those experts on at most four nodes, and dedupe means each node gets the token once. We'll take four (a slight overcount).
  5. If the four nodes are a uniform pick out of eight, our own node is one of them half the time, and that copy stays on NVLink. So on average a token crosses IB to 3.5 remote nodes. By symmetry, that's also what each node receives per token it owns.
  6. The dispatch activations are FP8 (1 byte).

Checking the NVLink assumption

[[AI: We waved NVLink away above; let's check it. When a token arrives over IB at a node, it lands on one GPU (the one with the same local index as the sender), which forwards it over NVLink to whichever GPUs on that node host the chosen experts. With eight experts over four nodes, each node gets two experts on average. A node has 32 experts, four per GPU, so two experts land on the same GPU 3/31 of the time: on average they need 2 − 3/31 ≈ 1.9 distinct GPUs. The landing GPU is one of them 1/8 of the time, so about 1.67 NVLink copies per node. The same holds for our own node, where the token starts on our GPU instead of arriving over IB.

So per token, per MoE layer, per pass, a GPU moves 4 × 1.67 × 7,168 ≈ 47.7 KB over NVLink against 25.1 KB over IB. NVLink carries almost twice the bytes, but at 160 GB/s (the rate the technical report says is achievable on H800) that takes 0.30 µs, against 0.50 µs for IB at 50 GB/s. NVLink runs at about 60% of IB's time, so it's not the bottleneck. This is the technical report's "3.2 experts per node" budget from the other direction: we use about 1.7 NVLink copies per IB copy, and could afford 3.2.]]

How EP scales

[[AI: Let's apply asymptotics to EP. Per token, per MoE layer, the routed experts do 3 matmuls (gate, up, down) of h × I each, for each of k experts, at 6 FLOPs per parameter forward and backward: 18·h·I·k FLOPs. EP moves h elements to n remote nodes, twice (dispatch and combine) in each of two passes, at 1 byte (FP8) plus 2 bytes (BF16): 6·h·n bytes. With peak FLOP rate F and IB bandwidth B, the ratio of communication time to routed-expert compute time is:

(6·h·n / B) ÷ (18·h·I·k / F) = n·F / (3·I·k·B)

The hidden size cancels. Making the model wider does nothing for EP unless the experts get wider too. (In the scaling family from earlier, they do: I grows with h, so EP gets relatively cheaper as models grow, just like PP.) What matters is expert width I, the number of experts per token k, how many nodes a token touches n, and the hardware's ratio F/B, which is 39,580 FLOPs per IB byte on H800.

For DSv3 (n = 3.5, I = 2,048, k = 8), EP takes 2.8× as long as the routed-expert matmuls it feeds: 5.4 s against 1.9 s. To break even, experts would need I ≈ 5,800, nearly three times wider. This is the tension in fine-grained MoE: splitting a fixed budget of active parameters into more, narrower experts keeps I·k constant, so compute doesn't change, but each token fans out to more nodes. Node-limited routing is DeepSeek's answer: it caps n at 4 no matter how many experts a token picks. And F/B is the reason GB200 changes the picture: inside an NVL72 domain, B is NVLink's 900 GB/s rather than IB's 50 GB/s.]]

ZeRO/FSDP

In the memory post, we said that ZeRO-3 would make DSv3 comms bound. Let's work this out.

In FSDP, we need to all-gather the parameters for forwards, all-gather for backwards and then reduce-scatter the final gradients. Each of these requires doing communication on order of the size of our parameters.

Low precision has some influence on the cost here.

[[AI: The key fact about these collectives is that a ring all-gather or reduce-scatter pushes almost the full size of the parameters through every GPU, no matter how many ways they're sharded. Sharding more ways saves memory, not bandwidth: conservation again, this time of bytes. With a hierarchical collective (reduce inside the node over NVLink first, then only 1/8 of the bytes cross IB) we can at least keep most of it off Infiniband.

We don't have to price the pure-FSDP world again: the fsdp side study found that sharding everything with ZeRO-3 (no EP, no PP) first fits at 128-way sharding and costs 27.8 s of sync per step, even when we're generous and gather the weights only once per step. So DeepSeek kept EP64 and PP16. Could they have layered ZeRO-3 on top of that? After PP and EP, one pipeline stage is roughly 1.07B non-expert parameters and 0.64B routed-expert parameters per GPU. DualPipe keeps two stages on every GPU (one for each direction the pipeline runs), so each stage is replicated 2 × 128 = 256 ways for the non-expert part and 2 × 2 = 4 ways for the experts (2,048 / (16 × 64) = 2). ZeRO-3 would gather the stage in use in BF16 for forward, again for backward, and reduce-scatter FP32 gradients, on every microbatch. PP is the problem here: it wants lots of microbatches, and at one 4,096-token sequence per microbatch each replica runs 15,360 / 128 = 120 of them per step. That's 4.9 GB of IB traffic per microbatch, 588 GB per step: 11.8 s of IB time, more than twice the 5.4 s EP already needs. Gathering in FP8 instead of BF16 only gets this down to 8.8 s.

But at speed of light, we already saw that EP alone makes Infiniband the binding resource: 5.4 s of IB against 3.4 s of compute. Every extra byte on IB goes straight onto the critical path, so ZeRO-3's 11.8 s would simply add to the step. It's a non-starter before we even get to overlap.

That raises the opposite question: DeepSeek does use ZeRO-1, so why do we tolerate its traffic? Because ZeRO-1 is free relative to plain data parallelism. A data-parallel all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather; ZeRO-1 does exactly that, except the optimizer step runs on the shard in between. Same bytes, rearranged. So the question is really why we tolerate data parallelism, and the answer is that we have no choice (2,048 GPUs have to be doing something) and it's cheap, because it happens once per step instead of once per microbatch. Reduce-scattering FP32 gradients and all-gathering BF16 parameters for both of a GPU's stages is 7.3 GB of IB traffic per GPU: 0.15 s, 3% of EP. That's the difference between traffic proportional to parameters (paid once per step) and traffic proportional to tokens or microbatches (paid over and over).]]

Pipeline parallelism

[[AI: Between pipeline stages, all that moves is the residual stream: one BF16 hidden vector per token forward (7,168 × 2 = 14,336 B), and its gradient backward. In DeepSeek's layout the eight GPUs of a node all belong to the same stage, so this crosses IB. Each GPU processes its replica's share of the batch, 15,360 × 4,096 / 128 = 491,520 tokens per step, and sends 28,672 B per token, which comes to 14.1 GB and 0.28 s.

The important part is how this scales. PP traffic per token grows linearly with the hidden size, but compute per token per layer grows quadratically with width, and a stage holds several layers (about four for DSv3). So as the model gets bigger, PP communication becomes relatively cheaper, both as width grows and as each stage gets more layers. (EP's bytes are also linear in the hidden size. What makes EP twenty times more expensive today is that it's paid at every MoE layer rather than every stage boundary, to 3.5 nodes instead of one, and twice in each pass: dispatch and combine.)]]

The pipeline bubble

[[AI: PP's bytes are cheap; its real cost is idle time. At the start of a step, later stages wait for the first microbatch to reach them, and at the end, earlier stages wait for the last backward to drain. With classic 1F1B scheduling that bubble is (PP − 1)(F + B), where F and B are one stage's forward and backward time for one microbatch. Each GPU does 120 microbatches of F + B work per step, and B ≈ 2F, so the bubble is 15 × 3F out of 120 × 3F: 15/120 = 12.5% of compute.

DualPipe feeds microbatches from both ends of the pipeline at once, which is why it keeps two stages per GPU. Its bubble is (PP/2 − 1)(F&B + B − 3W), where W is the weight-gradient half of the backward (W ≈ F) and F&B is a forward and a backward run overlapped. If that pair is compute-bound, F&B ≈ F + B = 3F, and the bubble is 7 × (3F + 2F − 3F) = 14F out of 360F: 3.9%. Notice this depends only on the number of stages and microbatches, not on the model's size: the bubble is a scheduling tax, and the fixed global batch is what bounds it. More microbatches would shrink it, but at one sequence per microbatch we already have as many as the batch allows.]]

Tensor and context parallelism

[[AI: DeepSeek uses neither, but it's worth pricing them anyway, because the roofline says why. Both put more than one GPU on a single sequence: tensor parallelism (TP) splits each layer's weights, context parallelism (CP) splits the sequence. That buys two things: less memory per GPU, and a cluster that can keep growing after the pipeline replicas run out of microbatches.

DSv3 at 2,048 GPUs needs neither. The memory post already fit, with about 18 GiB to spare. As for the batch: without TP or CP, the smallest unit of work is one 4,096-token sequence on one pipeline, and each replica runs 120 of them per step, for a 3.9% DualPipe bubble. The cluster could quadruple to 8,192 GPUs before that got uncomfortable (30 microbatches per replica, a bubble of 14F out of 90F = 15.6%). So the questions are what TP and CP would cost, and whether the memory they free could buy anything back.]]

Tensor parallelism

[[AI: In Megatron's layout for DSv3, TP splits the attention up-projections and output projection, the shared expert and the dense FFNs across the GPUs of a node; the routed experts stay under EP. With sequence parallelism, each of those blocks is bracketed by an all-gather of the residual stream going in and a reduce-scatter coming out: four collectives per layer forward, and four more backward. (Megatron also re-gathers inputs in the backward pass for the weight gradients, and the output head adds a pair of its own; we'll leave those out.) In each collective over t GPUs, a GPU receives the other t − 1 GPUs' share of the BF16 residual stream, so per token it owns, per layer, it receives:

8 × (t − 1) × 14,336 B

This stays inside the node, on NVLink rather than IB. But NVLink isn't idle: the NVLink check above found that EP's forwarding keeps it busy about 60% as long as IB, 3.2 s per step. At 160 GB/s, over 61 layers and 30,720 tokens per GPU:

Against 4.0 s of compute and 5.8 s of IB, TP4 and TP8 make NVLink the new bottleneck; TP across the whole node would roughly double the step. The H800's cut-down NVLink (400 GB/s, against the H100's 900) is a big part of this, and it's the cost DeepSeek means when its report says its memory savings let it train "without using costly Tensor Parallelism (TP)." TP2 fits under IB, at least in theory.

What would TP2 buy? At the memory post's configuration it takes the fullest GPU from 61.7 GiB to 36.5 GiB, mostly activations, since each GPU now holds half of every microbatch's tokens. That's enough to turn recompute off entirely (58.2 GiB), which saves 0.10 s of compute (0.21 s effective). So we'd pay 1.3 s of NVLink to save 0.1 to 0.2 s of compute. And the NVLink time isn't free even when there's slack: each all-gather has to land before the GEMM that consumes it, so hiding it takes overlap kernels with SMs of their own, and in the effective roofline SMs are the binding resource. The GEMMs get worse too: the shared expert's 2,048-wide intermediate becomes 1,024 at TP2 and 256 at TP8.

Asymptotically, TP moves 16·(t − 1)·h bytes per token per layer while the matmuls grow like h², so like PP, TP gets relatively cheaper as models get wider. But it gets more expensive as t grows: each GPU does 1/t of a token's compute, yet still receives (t − 1)/t of its activations, so communication per unit of compute grows like t − 1. That's why TP stays small and inside the fastest interconnect available.]]

Context parallelism

[[AI: CP splits each sequence across c GPUs. Everything except attention works token by token, so it runs unchanged; attention needs the keys and values of every earlier token. MLA can make this very cheap. A token's keys and values are up-projected from a 576-element latent (512 compressed plus 64 RoPE), 1,152 B in BF16. All-gathering the latents forward and reduce-scattering their gradients backward is 2 × (c − 1) × 1,152 B per token per layer: at CP2, 4.3 GB per GPU per step, 0.09 s even over IB. The catch is that every GPU then up-projects the whole sequence's latents rather than just its own, about 0.1 s of extra compute at CP2.

A generic ring-attention implementation only sees attention's inputs, so it passes the up-projected K and V instead: 128 heads × (192 + 128) × 2 B = 81,920 B per token, 71× the latent. Forward and backward, that's at least 2 × (c − 1) × 81,920 B per token per layer: 6.1 s on IB at CP2, or 1.9 s if the CP group stays inside a node. Whether CP is cheap depends on which tensor you send.

Either way, what CP buys is memory, and only activation memory: a CP group holds full copies of the weights, just like DP. CP2 halves activations just as TP2 does, which again is enough to turn recompute off (63.0 GiB), and again that's worth 0.10 s. CP earns its keep on long sequences, where one sequence's activations stop fitting on one GPU. Its asymptotics point the same way: the bytes per token are fixed by the KV width, but attention compute per token grows with sequence length, so the longer the sequence, the cheaper CP gets relative to the work it splits. At 4,096 tokens there's nothing for it to do.]]

The effective numbers move the two sides by very different amounts: IB loses 10%, but compute loses 42%. What overlap buys

Now we can use the roofline to make a decision. DualPipe overlaps communication with compute, but not for free: DeepSeek dedicates 20 of the H800's 132 SMs to communication, so compute runs 132/112 = 1.18× slower while it's overlapped. It also keeps two copies of the parameters. Is it worth it?

no overlap, 1F1BDualPipe
theoretical4.0 + 5.8 + 12.5% bubble = 10.3 smax(4.0 × 1.18, 5.8) + 3.9% bubble ≈ 6.0 s
effective6.9 + 6.4 + 12.5% bubble = 14.2 smax(6.9 × 1.18, 6.4) + 3.9% bubble ≈ 8.4 s

Both versions say overlap wins by a wide margin. But they disagree about what to do next. In the theoretical version the step is IB-bound, so the 20 SMs cost nothing (compute has slack to spare), and the only thing worth optimizing is IB bytes. In the effective version compute is the binding side, so those 20 SMs cost about 1.2 s per step, IB has about 1.7 s of slack, and a communication kernel that used fewer SMs would be worth more than one that moved fewer bytes. Same roofline, opposite advice: this is why the effective numbers are worth carrying.

Looking back, each of the three tools did one job. Speed of light ruled out ZeRO-3 without any engineering: even at peak, IB is already the bottleneck, so no amount of clever overlap could pay for another 11.8 s on it. Conservation let us price each piece without committing to a full parallel layout, and it also tells us which side we can negotiate with. The compute is fixed; the IB time is the product of our choices, and those choices swing it enormously (0.15 s for ZeRO-1 against 11.8 s for ZeRO-3). That's why we always try to hide communication under compute and never the other way round. Asymptotics told us which costs matter as models grow: matmul FLOPs grow quadratically with width while PP and EP traffic grow linearly, so a bigger model of the same shape gets relatively cheaper to communicate. (For EP, only if the experts grow too.)

When working complicated systems, we often look for simple reasoning mechanisms which are able to explain a (disproportionately) large amount of the system's behavior. In LLM training, I notice these three concepts are applied pervasively:

  1. Speed of light: When we know that no matter what we do, we cannot exceed some physical limitation, e.g., the number of FLOPs that the silicon on our GPU can support, or the bandwidth that our Infiniband network supports. Importantly, when we see that, even if we were at speed of light, some performance pathology exists, it shows us that we must take a different approach: no amount of incremental performance engineering will solve the problem. A related concept is roofline analysis.
  2. Conservation: We often need to reason about a large possibility space of how we configure our training run, e.g., parallelisms. However, for example, we know that no matter how we slice the pie of compute, at the end of the day we must still do that amount of compute. Given some assumed compute utilization, we can then tell how much communication we can afford: the amount of compute will never change, but we can choose different parallelization changes to adjust how much comms we do. (This is why we're always trying to make sure comms can be completely overlapped with compute, but not the other way around! The amount of compute can't be changed in an arch neutral way!)
  3. Asymptotics: The vocabulary LM head is a big deal at small scale, because your vocabulary is quite large. However, vocabulary size doesn't change as we scale up, whereas parameter count does. If we are planning on scaling, we should focus on the parts of our system that also scale, as constant costs asymptote to zero as you scale. (That's not to say constant costs cannot give material performance improvements! But Amdahl's law tells us the amount of improvement we can get via these optimizations diminishes.)
