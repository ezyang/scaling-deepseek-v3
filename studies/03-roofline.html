Working the roofline for DeepSeek-V3 on Hopper

In part two, we looked at how our choices of parallelism, activation checkpointing and low precision influence how much memory we need to train DeepSeek-V3, and found"Found" is perhaps too strong a word: it is not a coincidence that if you keep the architecture fixed and look for a configuration that works well for it on the hardware DeepSeek trained on, you end up with the configuration DeepSeek picked. An interesting and important operating mode is codesign, where we (infra) can have input into aspects of the architecture (e.g., expert sparsity) so that it runs better. This is a discussion unto itself, which we will delay for later. a configuration that let us fit it on a 2048 H800 cluster. However, you often have some choice in how exactly you get the model to fit, and we'd like to choose the configuration that maximizes our useful FLOPs/sec. For example, our first question was whether to use FSDP (ZeRO-3) or pipeline parallelism. It is quite painful to implement pipeline parallelism, so we would really like to decide on a strategy without having to build both systems and benchmark them on a full-size cluster. And even if we did build and run the benchmark, how would we know that we hadn't made a mistake that invalidated the comparison?

The classic tool for answering questions like this is a roofline analysis: at the end of the day, the number of FLOPs we need to run is fixed, and so the most important factor of our system's performance will be its distributed communication (much in the same way the most important factor of kernel performance is if it is compute or bandwidth bound). A roofline analysis will tell us that FSDP is a bad idea for DSv3, because it will be communications bound on InfiniBand bandwidth. If we consider speed of light, where we assume our compute and communications go as fast as physically possible (according to the hardware spec sheets), these calculations are simple enough to be done with pencil and paper.

The goals for this part are two-fold. First, we will walk through the speed of light rooflines for DeepSeek-V3 on Hopper. But second, and more importantly, I want to explain the underlying reasoning behind the approximations we will use in this analysis. For example, the 6ND formula is really useful, but it can also feel a bit like magic the first time you encounter it. I want to explain it in enough detail that you could derive it from first principles and also tell when you shouldn't use it.

What's a roofline?

This section is standard exposition, so you can skip it if you have already seen it. You might also consider reading Making Deep Learning Go Brrrr From First Principles or All About Rooflines.

The term roofline originally comes from the Williams, Waterman and Patterson paper in the context of understanding program performance when memory bandwidth was the constraining factor. Its name comes from the classic shape of the graph you get when you plot arithmetic intensity (the number of FLOPs you perform per byte you read from or write to memory) against attainable FLOP/s.

Roofline analysis is a natural thing to do when authoring GPU kernels. The roofline tells you if a kernel is compute bound or memory bound. When a kernel is memory bound, making the compute run faster is useless, since after you finish your compute you will simply idle waiting for bytes to show up from HBM. Instead, fusing multiple kernels together helps you amortize the cost of HBM reads over more compute, helping push you into the compute bound regime. Conversely, we don't usually worry about fusing GEMMs together (modulo attention or low batch inference), because matrix multiplication naturally has a high arithmetic intensity and is compute-bound in most regimes. It's also fairly difficult to "fuse" two GEMMs together (or even usefully fuse into a GEMM prologue), because input tiles are used multiple times when computing the GEMM: to avoid repeatedly recomputing an input tile, you need to store it somewhere that all of your SMs can access, and has enough space to store everything you need. Usually HBM is the most convenient thing that actually fits. The fact that Flash Attention can keep the score matrix on-chip without going to HBM is something of a minor miracle.

We can also do a roofline analysis on an entire distributed train step. However, there are two big differences. First, the HBM is no longer the bottleneck: instead, the bottleneck will usually be your cross-node bandwidthWell, technically, Blackwell's NVL72 spans across nodes, but it's usually useful to think of your NVL domain as a "node" in this analysis., e.g., your InfiniBand bandwidth. Second, the amount of communication a distributed training setup performs depends on your choice of parallelization—in contrast, the amount of FLOPs you need to do, on a fixed architecture, is fixed, no matter how you parallelize it! A good analogy is this: imagine you have a pizza pie representing how much work (FLOPs) you need to do. No matter how you slice the pie, reducing the individual amount of work a given node needs to do, all of the slices still add up to the original pie. The analogy works less well when you think about recompute conjuring more pizza to eat—a good thing in real life, and a bad thing in distributed training!

In both cases, the rule of thumb is that you want to be compute bound, since that usually means you are efficiently utilizing the hardware resources you have available. In distributed training, this means you want your compute to take longer than your communications, since a well-implemented trainer will manage to overlap the comms and make them effectively free.Ha ha ha, actually, it's not free lmao. If you don't have enough compute to cover up your comms, you probably want to change something (your architecture, or your parallelism if your architecture is fixed) to put yourself back in the good compute bound regime.There are also architectural changes you might make which make it harder to be compute-bound, especially optimizations for compute efficiency. For example, switching your GEMMs to low precision can exacerbate comms-boundedness, since it makes your GEMMs run a lot faster without necessarily reducing the amount of communications you need to do.

We've promised that we would show that ZeRO-3 makes you comms bound for the original DeepSeek-V3 training setup. Here's how we will figure this out:

  1. We'll count the FLOPs a single training step takes. Dividing by the speed of light FLOP/s of our hardware gives the time we spend in compute.
  2. For each parallelism, we'll count the bytes it moves per link and what bandwidth it uses (NVLink inside a node, InfiniBand between nodes). Dividing the per-link bytes by the speed of light byte/s on that network link gives us the time we spend in comms.
  3. By comparing these times, we can tell if we are compute bound (compute time is larger) or comms bound (one of the links' comms times is larger—the bottleneck, typically InfiniBand).

How many FLOPs do we need to do?

Short answer: The classic training FLOPs approximation is 6ND, where N is the number of activated parameters in the model and D is the number of tokens (either the total number of trainable tokens, giving the total amount of FLOPs for the training run, or simply the number of tokens in a global batch, giving the total amount of FLOPs for a single train stepIt's fairly typical to treat the global batch size as a fixed quantity that can't be changed, as it is typically set by science: beyond a critical batch size, increasing batch size yields diminishing returns on optimization efficiency.). This formula is indifferent to our choice of parallelism: we always have to run these FLOPs somewhere, one way or another, no matter how we have split up our work.

Long answer: Where the heck does the 6ND approximation come from? At its heart, it is an approximation based off of the idea that your non-attention matmuls take the bulk of the FLOPs in your model (in part 1, we used this idea to justify treating the matmuls specially in our architecture diagram). The number of FLOPs per token that a learned linear transformation computes scales linearly with the parameters of that matmul. We can see this by assigning each FLOP we need to do to a parameter in this diagram:

every weight does one multiply and one add per token: 2 FLOPs per parameter

So you derive 6ND by multiplying N (the parameter countTechnically, we shouldn't include non-matmul params like the RMSNorm parameters or the router bias in the parameter count, but these parameters are proportionally very small and don't make a big difference either way.) by D (number of tokens) and then scaling it by 6 (two FLOPs per matmul times three matmuls total: one in forwards, two in backwards computing the gradient of the input and the gradient of the weight.)

Why is this an appropriate approximation to make? We are making an argument about asymptotic behavior when we scale compute. However, upon close reflection, you might think it a bit strange to reason about the asymptotic behavior of DeepSeek-V3: after all, there's only one size for this model—671B! What exactly are you scaling?

In pretraining research, it is natural to think of an architecture as actually a family of architectures of different sizes, designed to be as similar to each other as possible, so that we can do science on the smaller sizes, fit scaling laws, and then extrapolate them to the expensive hero run. What happens to the FLOPs of a model as we increase compute, trying to keep the architecture otherwise the same? Given some compute budget C, here is a standard recipe for defining this family (for example, this matches this scaling ladder design in the MAI-Thinking tech report):

  • Per Chinchilla, we will choose to keep tokens-per-parameter (TPP) fixed, so that our parameter count and number of tokens grow proportionally.
  • We will choose to keep the aspect ratio (AR) of our model fixed, so that the depth and width of our model are proportionally related as we increase parameter count.
  • We will choose to keep our compute allocation between attention and MLP fixed, so the hidden size and intermediate size (the MLP hidden size) are proportionally related as we increase the width of our network.
  • We will choose to keep the MoE sparsity structure fixed: so the number of experts and how many experts we route to stay fixed even as compute grows.

When we increase depth, all of the transformer block FLOPs increase uniformly linearly with depth. However, when we increase width, while non-matmul compute increases linearly, the learned linear transformations (matmuls that are activation × weight) increase quadratically, since both the input and output spaces get bigger. This means that, in the limit, learned linear transformation FLOPs dominate everything else.

There is a caveat: inside attention, there are attention activation matmuls that don't (directly) take a weight matrix. These scale more slowly than the parameter count (linearly, not quadratically, in width), but they also scale with sequence length. It's common to pretrain with some fixed sequence length, and then only scale the sequence length in some midtraining for context length. This commonly results in a situation where the 6ND approximation is good for pretraining (sequence length not too long) but bad for midtraining (long sequence length means attention is now a bottleneck). The diagram below shows us how the proportion of FLOPs changes as we scale up our compute and sequence length:The LM head (yellow) segment of this graph is worth a comment. The compute needed by the LM head scales with your vocabulary size, which is typically fixed throughout your entire scaling ladder. So on small models, the LM head is an extremely expensive and a very noticeable part of your compute, but because its compute is only linear in hidden size, its share asymptotes to nothing in the limit (like the non-matmul operations).

Expert parallelism

How much time do the dispatch and combine communications take in the expert parallel region? This is a bit more annoying to do in the Hopper era, because our EP spans across nodes, rather than being in a single NVL72 domain as would be the case in GB200/GB300. But it is not too complicated, and we know directionally what the roofline should look like, as the DeepSeek-V3 technical report specifies that they have roughly a 1:1 computation-to-communication ratio (e.g., as seen in their published profile traces) due to the cross-node EP communication. Let's assemble our assumptions for our roofline:

  • We'll assume uniform routing: the nodes a token is routed to are a uniform pick among the nodes of the EP group (including our own).
  • We will assume use of deduplication. The idea is that if multiple GPUs on the same node require the same token, we don't have to broadcast it multiple times on IB; instead, we can dispatch it once and then use NVLink to locally distribute it to the correct GPU. Additionally, DSv3 has a routing constraint that a maximum of four nodes can be routed to, so our worst case InfiniBand cost is four copies of the token's hidden vector.
  • Infiniband is full-duplex, meaning that we can simultaneously transmit data at the link rate in both directions simultaneously. Thus we will analyze the overall communication needed simply by looking at how much data any given node gets on the receive side.
  • We will assume that the local NVLink is fast enough relative to Infiniband that we can neglect its bandwidth for the purpose of this calculation. It's only about 3.2× faster on H800 (160 GB/s vs 50 GB/s), but the original DeepSeek-v3 technical report notes that "communications via IB and NVLink are fully overlapped, and each token can efficiently select an average of 3.2 experts per node without incurring additional overhead from NVLink." With four nodes for eight experts, on average there will only be two experts per node the token needs to be routed to.

In practice, we will be able to do a little better than having to send four copies of each token's hidden vector over InfiniBand, since if the expert is colocated on the same node no IB communication is needed. A nice and simple ratio is to say we need to send each token's hidden vector to 3.5 remote nodes, which is the expected number of communications you'd get if you picked exactly four of the eight nodes uniformly at random per token.This is technically an overestimate, since DeepSeek-V3 limits routing to at most four nodes, not exactly four. Specifically, the router picks the four nodes whose two best experts score highest, then takes the top eight experts among those nodes. This means that potentially one of the four selected nodes could end up hosting none of the active experts. The graph has an option to switch to DeepSeek-V3 routing: about one token in 22 touches only three nodes, pushing the mean down to 3.46, assuming independent, uniform affinity scores.

ZeRO/FSDP

In the memory post, we said that ZeRO-3 would make DSv3 comms bound. Let's work this out.

In FSDP, we need to all-gather the parameters for forwards, all-gather for backwards and then reduce-scatter the final gradients. Each of these requires doing communication on order of the size of our parameters.

Low precision has some influence on the cost here.

One gather is 24.1 GB of IB traffic per GPU, 0.48 s, and the FP32 gradient reduce-scatter is twice that. What matters is how many gathers a step needs. ZeRO-3 gathers each layer's weights for its forward, drops them, and gathers them again for its backward (keeping them is the memory it set out to save), and it does this for every microbatch.

Without PP, activation memory sets the number of microbatches. PP divides a GPU's activations by the number of stages. FSDP, like any data parallelism, divides nothing: each GPU stashes activations for all 61 layers. With DeepSeek's recompute policy from the memory post, one 4,096-token sequence is 38.9 GiB of stash (35.9 GiB in the layers, 3.0 GiB of logits for the loss). Two sequences would be 77.8 GiB, and with the 4.3 GiB of sharded weights, gradients and optimizer state that's over the H800's 79.65 GiB before any working memory. So each GPU runs its 30,720 tokens as 7.5 microbatches of one sequence each: 15 gathers per step, and 8.2 s of IB even if we charge the reduce-scatter only once. (That's generous: accumulating gradients unsharded between microbatches would take more memory than the GPU has.) On top of EP's 5.4 s, that's 13.6 s of IB against 6.8 s of compute, 2× over. Gathering in FP8 (with its scales) roughly halves the gathers but leaves 10.1 s of IB, and if two sequences did squeeze in, halving the gathers again, it would be 10.0 s. And the BF16 peak is the generous price here: FP8 GEMMs only make compute faster and the gap wider.

FSDP's best move is to break the link between microbatches and gathers: recompute everything, stashing only each layer's input (14 KiB per token per layer, 25.0 GiB for all 30,720 tokens, with the loss computed a sequence at a time), and run the whole step as one microbatch. Now each layer is gathered once for forward and once for backward (the recomputed forward reuses the backward's gather), and ZeRO-3 costs 1.9 s. But full recompute isn't free. Every layer runs its forward twice, a third more compute (8ND: 9.1 s), and the replayed MoE layers dispatch and combine again, another 2.7 s of EP. IB comes to 5.4 + 2.7 + 1.9 = 10.0 s against 9.1 s of compute: still IB-bound, now by 10%. (Counting the attention core would push compute to about 10.4 s and tip it to compute-bound, but it doesn't make the step any shorter.)

So under FSDP, the step can't beat 10 s at speed of light. The pipelined layout, which we price next, needs 5.8 s of IB against the same 6.8 s of compute, so it's compute-bound at 6.8 s. FSDP makes the step about 45% longer to escape a pipeline bubble that DualPipe holds to 3.9%.

That raises the opposite question: DeepSeek does use ZeRO-1, so why do we tolerate its traffic? Because ZeRO-1 is free relative to plain data parallelism. A data-parallel all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather; ZeRO-1 does exactly that, except the optimizer step runs on the shard in between. Same bytes, rearranged. So the question is really why we tolerate data parallelism, and the answer is that we have no choice (2,048 GPUs have to be doing something) and it's cheap, because it happens once per step instead of once per microbatch. Reduce-scattering FP32 gradients and all-gathering BF16 parameters for both of a GPU's stages is 7.3 GB of IB traffic per GPU: 0.15 s, 3% of EP. That's the difference between traffic proportional to parameters (paid once per step) and traffic proportional to tokens or microbatches (paid over and over).]]

Pipeline parallelism

[[AI: Between pipeline stages, all that moves is the residual stream: one BF16 hidden vector per token forward (7,168 × 2 = 14,336 B), and its gradient backward. In DeepSeek's layout the eight GPUs of a node all belong to the same stage, so this crosses IB. Each GPU processes its replica's share of the batch, 15,360 × 4,096 / 128 = 491,520 tokens per step, and sends 28,672 B per token, which comes to 14.1 GB and 0.28 s.

The important part is how this scales. PP traffic per token grows linearly with the hidden size, but compute per token per layer grows quadratically with width, and a stage holds several layers (about four for DSv3). So as the model gets bigger, PP communication becomes relatively cheaper, both as width grows and as each stage gets more layers. (EP's bytes are also linear in the hidden size. What makes EP twenty times more expensive today is that it's paid at every MoE layer rather than every stage boundary, to 3.5 nodes instead of one, and twice in each pass: dispatch and combine.)]]

The pipeline bubble

[[AI: PP's bytes are cheap; its real cost is idle time. At the start of a step, later stages wait for the first microbatch to reach them, and at the end, earlier stages wait for the last backward to drain. With classic 1F1B scheduling that bubble is (PP − 1)(F + B), where F and B are one stage's forward and backward time for one microbatch. Each GPU does 120 microbatches of F + B work per step, and B ≈ 2F, so the bubble is 15 × 3F out of 120 × 3F: 15/120 = 12.5% of compute.

DualPipe feeds microbatches from both ends of the pipeline at once, which is why it keeps two stages per GPU. Its bubble is (PP/2 − 1)(F&B + B − 3W), where W is the weight-gradient half of the backward (W ≈ F) and F&B is a forward and a backward run overlapped. If that pair is compute-bound, F&B ≈ F + B = 3F, and the bubble is 7 × (3F + 2F − 3F) = 14F out of 360F: 3.9%. Notice this depends only on the number of stages and microbatches, not on the model's size: the bubble is a scheduling tax, and the fixed global batch is what bounds it. More microbatches would shrink it, but at one sequence per microbatch we already have as many as the batch allows.]]

Tensor and context parallelism

[[AI: DeepSeek uses neither, but it's worth pricing them anyway, because the roofline says why. Both put more than one GPU on a single sequence: tensor parallelism (TP) splits each layer's weights, context parallelism (CP) splits the sequence. That buys two things: less memory per GPU, and a cluster that can keep growing after the pipeline replicas run out of microbatches.

DSv3 at 2,048 GPUs needs neither. The memory post already fit, with about 18 GiB to spare. As for the batch: without TP or CP, the smallest unit of work is one 4,096-token sequence on one pipeline, and each replica runs 120 of them per step, for a 3.9% DualPipe bubble. The cluster could quadruple to 8,192 GPUs before that got uncomfortable (30 microbatches per replica, a bubble of 14F out of 90F = 15.6%). So the questions are what TP and CP would cost, and whether the memory they free could buy anything back.]]

The GEMMs get worse too: the shared expert's 2,048-wide intermediate becomes 1,024 at TP2 and 256 at TP8.

Asymptotically, TP moves 16·(t − 1)·h bytes per token per layer while the matmuls grow like h², so like PP, TP gets relatively cheaper as models get wider. But it gets more expensive as t grows: each GPU does 1/t of a token's compute, yet still receives (t − 1)/t of its activations, so communication per unit of compute grows like t − 1. That's why TP stays small and inside the fastest interconnect available.]]

Context parallelism

[[AI: CP splits each sequence across c GPUs. Everything except attention works token by token, so it runs unchanged; attention needs the keys and values of every earlier token. MLA can make this very cheap. A token's keys and values are up-projected from a 576-element latent (512 compressed plus 64 RoPE), 1,152 B in BF16. All-gathering the latents forward and reduce-scattering their gradients backward is 2 × (c − 1) × 1,152 B per token per layer: at CP2, 4.3 GB per GPU per step, 0.09 s even over IB. The catch is that every GPU then up-projects the whole sequence's latents rather than just its own, about 0.2 s of extra compute at CP2.

A generic ring-attention implementation only sees attention's inputs, so it passes the up-projected K and V instead: 128 heads × (192 + 128) × 2 B = 81,920 B per token, 71× the latent. Forward and backward, that's at least 2 × (c − 1) × 81,920 B per token per layer: 6.1 s on IB at CP2, or 1.9 s if the CP group stays inside a node. Whether CP is cheap depends on which tensor you send.

Either way, what CP buys is memory, and only activation memory: a CP group holds full copies of the weights, just like DP. CP2 halves activations just as TP2 does, which again is enough to turn recompute off (63.0 GiB), and again that's worth 0.2 s. CP earns its keep on long sequences, where one sequence's activations stop fitting on one GPU. Its asymptotics point the same way: the bytes per token are fixed by the KV width, but attention compute per token grows with sequence length, so the longer the sequence, the cheaper CP gets relative to the work it splits. At 4,096 tokens there's nothing for it to do.]]

What overlap buys

Now we can use the roofline to make a decision. DualPipe overlaps communication with compute, but not for free: DeepSeek dedicates 20 of the H800's 132 SMs to communication, so compute runs 132/112 = 1.18× slower while it's overlapped. It also keeps two copies of the parameters. Is it worth it?

Both versions say overlap wins by a wide margin, and they agree about what to do next. Compute is the binding side, so the 20 SMs cost real time (1.4 s per step at the BF16 peak, 1.2 s effective) while IB sits idle part of the time, and a communication kernel that used fewer SMs would be worth more than one that moved fewer bytes. What the effective numbers change is how much room there is: IB's slack is 3.6 s at the peaks but only 1.7 s in practice, so the bytes can't grow much before the advice flips.

Looking back, each of the three tools did one job. Speed of light ruled out FSDP without any engineering: even at IB's full line rate, run DeepSeek's way it puts 13.6 s of IB into every step, twice the step's compute at the BF16 peak, and its best variant still can't beat 10 s, so no amount of clever overlap could rescue it. Conservation let us price each piece without committing to a full parallel layout, and it also tells us which side we can negotiate with. The compute is fixed; the IB time is the product of our choices, and those choices swing it enormously (0.15 s for ZeRO-1 against 8.2 s for ZeRO-3). That's why we always try to hide communication under compute and never the other way round. Asymptotics told us which costs matter as models grow: matmul FLOPs grow quadratically with width while PP and EP traffic grow linearly, so a bigger model of the same shape gets relatively cheaper to communicate. (For EP, only if the experts grow too.)

When working complicated systems, we often look for simple reasoning mechanisms which are able to explain a (disproportionately) large amount of the system's behavior. In LLM training, I notice these three concepts are applied pervasively:

  1. Speed of light: When we know that no matter what we do, we cannot exceed some physical limitation, e.g., the number of FLOPs that the silicon on our GPU can support, or the bandwidth that our Infiniband network supports. Importantly, when we see that, even if we were at speed of light, some performance pathology exists, it shows us that we must take a different approach: no amount of incremental performance engineering will solve the problem. A related concept is roofline analysis.
  2. Conservation: We often need to reason about a large possibility space of how we configure our training run, e.g., parallelisms. However, for example, we know that no matter how we slice the pie of compute, at the end of the day we must still do that amount of compute. Given some assumed compute utilization, we can then tell how much communication we can afford: the amount of compute will never change, but we can choose different parallelization changes to adjust how much comms we do. (This is why we're always trying to make sure comms can be completely overlapped with compute, but not the other way around! The amount of compute can't be changed in an arch neutral way!)
  3. Asymptotics: The vocabulary LM head is a big deal at small scale, because your vocabulary is quite large. However, vocabulary size doesn't change as we scale up, whereas parameter count does. If we are planning on scaling, we should focus on the parts of our system that also scale, as constant costs asymptote to zero as you scale. (That's not to say constant costs cannot give material performance improvements! But Amdahl's law tells us the amount of improvement we can get via these optimizations diminishes.)
