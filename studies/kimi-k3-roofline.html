Kimi K3 on GB300: the roofline, sheets only

DRAFT (unlisted study), written by an AI. Sheets only, no diagrams; the sheets, the sweeps and all of the prose were generated by Fable (Claude) from the author’s brief and have not had a human edit pass — see the footnote.1

The question: with a fixed global batch, GB300 NVL72 hardware and a fixed mixed-precision recipe, what is the smallest cluster that trains Kimi K3 at (close to) its best tokens/s per GPU? Part 3’s method, run again: count the FLOPs, count the bytes each parallelism moves per link, divide by the link’s rate, and check that compute covers the communication. Everything here is a speed-of-light number: spec peaks, perfect overlap, no kernel inefficiency. The sheets are live, as in part 3: the formula column is the program, blue values are knobs, and every sheet’s header downloads the whole page as one workbook.

Assumptions carried from the brief: forced-balanced routing (every EP rank receives exactly S · k tokens per sequence, as MoonEP guarantees); a rank-aware pipeline cache for the attention residual, so each layer output crosses a pipeline boundary once; cross-rank activation offload, so the in-flight activations of 1F1B are averaged across pipeline ranks; and two activation-checkpointing policies, A (recompute everything that is not a matmul) and B (recompute everything except SDPA, KDA and the routed experts).

A common variant uses 64 of the 72 GPUs in a domain (set NVL to 64 and the sweeps below re-run on 64-GPU domains). Per GPU nothing changes: weights, gradients and activations per GPU depend on PP · EP and the cluster size, not on the domain, and the EP traffic per token moves by under 1 %. What changes is the grain: with FP32 gradients and states the no-offload minimum is still three domains, now 192 GPUs instead of 216 (PP 24 × EP 8 under V-Min, 7,247 tokens/s per GPU), and the four-domain cluster is 256 instead of 288. The two-domain floor is where the two differ: 144 GPUs with host offload fit every gradient and optimizer policy, 128 do not fit the all-FP32 one, because each of them carries 12.5 % more MXFP8 weights and gradients. The one real advantage of 64 is not in this sheet: 896 experts divide by 64 (14 per rank) and not by 72, so MoonEP’s redundant-expert slots are needed for balance at 72 and not at 64.

The architecture from the released config and modeling code (the shapes that are only in the code: the KDA decay bottleneck and the gated norm). Compute

The recipe, as briefed: MXFP8 for the attention projections, the dense FFN and shared experts, the routed experts and their latent projections, and the LM head; the routers run BF16 with FP32 logits out; both attention cores in BF16. Routed experts could run their weights in MXFP4, K3’s post-training QAT format, but this sheet keeps them in MXFP8; πfp4 is listed for the reader who wants to try it. Policy A recomputes only vector work, which 6ND never counted, so it adds no time here; policy B replays every non-expert, non-core matmul once in backward, a 2ND term on the replayed parameters. The KDA core is priced per token from its chunkwise form: three dh×dh state matmuls per head plus the intra-chunk terms; it is small, and this is the roughest line on the page.

Expert parallelism over NVLink

Dispatch and combine stay on NVLink. Per token and MoE layer the latent goes out MXFP8 (with one scale byte per 32) and comes back BF16, to k · (1 − 1/EP) remote ranks without dedup; K3 saves only the dispatch input and re-dispatches in backward, so the forward dispatch is paid three times per step rather than twice. The EP ranks also hold identical non-expert slices, whose gradients reduce and whose updated weights gather over the same NVLink.

ZeRO-1 over the scale-out network

Optimizer states are sharded over every replica of a slice. The per-step cost is a reduce-scatter of the gradients (at gB) and a BF16 all-gather of the updated weights of each GPU’s IB share, plus Muon’s Newton–Schulz gather (each rank fetches the remote shards of the matrices it owns). When a slice has exactly one replica the term is zero: that is the situation at the default cluster size, and it is why the smallest cluster is also the one with no scale-out optimizer traffic at all.

Pipeline parallelism

A pipeline boundary carries the AttnRes block, 12 layer outputs per token with the rank-aware cache (each output sent once), rather than one residual: 12× DeepSeek-V3’s per-token boundary traffic. It is charged to the scale-out link even when the whole pipeline fits inside a domain. The bubble is plain 1F1B without virtual stages; with 100+ microbatches per pipeline at the default size it is a few percent, and virtual stages would cut it further at larger clusters.

Other schedules

The rows above are plain 1F1B. Each schedule changes two cells: the microbatches in flight (memory) and the bubble (time). Interleaving with V virtual stages divides the bubble by V but roughly doubles the in-flight count; splitting the weight gradient off the backward (zero-bubble, ZB-H1) fills a further two thirds of the bubble at the same memory. The V-shaped schedules of “Pipeline Parallelism with Controllable Memory” (arXiv 2405.15362) put two chunks per rank in a V and trade bubble for memory: V-Min at a third of 1F1B’s peak, V-Half at half, V-ZB at 1F1B’s peak with no bubble at all. Bubble shares use the paper’s unit-time (F = B = W) counts; memory is the paper’s exact per-rank peak for the V schedules and the rank-averaged count for the interleaved ones. The sweep tables at the end consider every schedule.

Offloading to the host

Is offloading activations always good? At speed of light it is a bandwidth question: every stashed byte crosses to Grace memory and back, and that traffic must hide inside the compute. φ is the share of the stash the host link can carry per step; over 100 % the whole stash can live on the host and only two microbatches of this rank’s layers stay resident. At the default layout φ is under 100 % for policy A — its MXFP8 stash is bigger than the host link can move inside the compute — and about even for policy B, so offload is a partial relief for A and a full one for B. The optimizer state is a separate question: offload it after the step and prefetch it during the last microbatch’s backward; βpf is the bandwidth that window demands. The sweep’s offload rows use these cells: resident activations = A − min(φ, 1) · (A − Amin), states resident only at the step’s end, both the mid-step and end-of-step peaks checked, host capacity checked.

Putting it together

At the default layout (216 GPUs: PP 12 × EP 18, a single replica, so no ZeRO-1 traffic at all) plain 1F1B is too fat for policy A (107 % of device memory) and fits policy B (91.4 %). ZB-V-Min holds 5 microbatches in flight instead of 6.5 and brings policy A in at 292 GB, running at 7.23 K tokens/s per GPU against a compute-only ceiling of 7.36 K; the whole loss is its 1.76 % bubble. Policy B pays its replay and lands at 6.26 K under V-Half. Host offload: the link can carry 70.6 % of policy A’s stash inside the compute and 141 % of policy B’s, so offloading everything is only possible for B; prefetching the optimizer state in one microbatch’s backward would need 650 % of the host bandwidth, so it has to start 7 microbatches early. Reading the tables: once a zero-bubble schedule fits, tokens/s per GPU sits at the compute-only ceiling at every cluster size, because the only per-step fixed cost left, ZeRO-1 traffic, is overlapped (its share of compute does grow with cluster size, and the † marks where it or the EP traffic finally exceeds compute). Below that size the memory-lean V schedules give up a percent or two of bubble to fit. So “the minimum number of GB300s that maximizes tokens/s per GPU” is the smallest cluster at which the preferred policy fits: with FP32 gradients and states and nothing offloaded, three domains (216 GPUs) for either policy, A under ZB-V-Min at 7,233 and B under V-Half 14 % slower; BF16 gradients and states take the no-offload minimum to two domains; host offload takes every combination to two domains (144), with the ‡ reminder that the optimizer prefetch must start several microbatches early. Two domains is the floor for any of this: below it the MXFP8 weights and gradients alone overflow. Fits above 95 % of device memory should be read as “on the line”: this roofline holds no communication buffers, no fragmentation, no vision tower.
