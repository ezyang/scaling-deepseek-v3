Memory: a Blackwell case study

The same model on 3.5× the memory.PyTorch reports 276.6 GiB of device memory on a GB300 and 184.0 GiB on a GB200 (the spec sheets say "288 GB" and "186 GB"). As in the Hopper post we round down to a yardstick, 276 and 184 GiB, since a roofline needs headroom for the intermediates it doesn't model.

AI disclosure: the text of this post was drafted by Fable from the author's notes, the model, and a source check of Megatron-Bridge, Megatron-Core, Transformer Engine and the MLPerf v6.0 submissions; it has not yet had its human edit pass. The figures are the same reviewed widgets as the previous post, extended for Blackwell.

In the previous post we squeezed DeepSeek-V3 onto 80 GiB with PP8 under DualPipeV, EP64, ZeRO-1, the paper's recompute policy and FP8 stashes, and were left with 18 GiB to spare. Blackwell multiplies the budget by two to three and a half, and NVIDIA publishes the configurations it trains DeepSeek-V3 with on that hardware. This post redoes the accounting for those configurations. It is shorter than the Hopper post because the levers are the same; what changes is which of them you get to put down. Blackwell also comes with a different training stack, Megatron rather than DeepSeek's in-house one, so along the way we meet Megatron's pipeline schedule, its recompute knobs, and its MXFP8 recipe.

Which configuration?

"NVIDIA's DeepSeek-V3 configuration" is several configurations, from two different kinds of source, and it pays to keep them apart.

The Megatron-Bridge performance archive. NVIDIA's Megatron-Bridge repository publishes a performance summary with one DeepSeek-V3 row per system per container release. These are throughput benchmarks: forced-balanced routing, token-dropless, no evaluation, no convergence target. The 26.06 rows, all 256 GPUs, MXFP8, sequence length 4096, micro-batch 1:

  • DGX-GB300: TP1 / PP2 / VP8 / EP32, global batch 4096 — 6,338 tokens/s/GPU (6,422 at global batch 15,360).
  • DGX-GB200: TP1 / PP4 / VP4 / EP64 — 4,969 tokens/s/GPU.
  • DGX-B300 (eight-GPU NVLink domains, not NVL72): TP1 / PP8 / EP8, micro-batch 2 — 3,541 tokens/s/GPU.

MLPerf Training v6.0. MLCommons added DeepSeek-V3 pretraining as a benchmark in 2026, and the June results include ten closed-division submissions, all on Blackwell. This is a different workload: real routing on C4, global batch at least 15,360 sequences, MTP on, evaluation after every step, and a validation-loss target. NVIDIA's 256-GPU GB300 submission (result 6.0-0100, 33.4 minutes to train) runs TP1 / PP4 / VP4 / EP32 with no activation recompute; its GB200 submissions run TP2 / PP4 / VP4 / EP32 with the dense MLPs recomputed.Configs and logs: mlcommons/training_results_v6.0, NVIDIA/benchmarks/deepseekv3_671b/implementations/theia_ngpu256_ngc26.04_nemo/. The GB300 config's own header points back at the Megatron-Bridge workload configs as its source. From the logs, the 256-GPU GB300 run sustains about 6,245 tokens/s/GPU in steady state, in line with the archive's 26.06 row.

The public GB200 guide. Megatron-LM's repository carries a reproduce guide for an older GB200 configuration: PP8 / EP32, a 32-chunk layout, and two recompute modules. It trains without MTP.

The two GB300 configurations share one recipe, which is what this post models: MXFP8 on every GEMM including attention and the LM-head projection, FP8 parameter gather, Megatron's distributed optimizer with fp32 master weights and bf16 Adam moments, a bf16 gradient buffer, the pipeline layout Et*4|(t*4|)*14tmL, no recompute, and the 1F1B all-to-all overlap schedule.Bridge v0.5.0 DEEPSEEK_V3_PRETRAIN_CONFIG_GB300_FP8_MX_V2 (the archive row) and the MLPerf config_GB300_64x4x240xtp1pp4ep32cp1. The recipe has been stable from Bridge v0.5.0 through the current tree. We take the archive's PP2 / VP8 row as the reference configuration, because it is the one with the fewest levers pulled, and show the MLPerf PP4 / VP4 configuration alongside it.

Here is the whole bill again, before any parallelism, at Megatron's conventions. Two things differ from the Hopper bill: the cluster is 256 GPUs rather than 2048, so every "÷ DP" is eight times weaker, and the gradient buffer is bf16 rather than fp32 (Megatron's perf recipes set grad_reduce_in_fp32=False; the fp32 copy of a rank's gradient shard exists only inside the optimizer step, after the activations are gone).

Pipeline parallelism, Megatron style

Megatron does not use DualPipeV. Its production schedule is interleaved 1F1B: each pipeline rank owns not one contiguous chunk of the model but VP of them ("virtual pipeline" stages), dealt out round-robin, chunk i on rank i mod PP. Where DualPipeV folds the model into a V so that rank 0 holds both ends, interleaving wraps it around the ranks like a spiral. The reference configuration is PP2 with VP8: two physical ranks, sixteen chunks.

Megatron describes the split with a small layout language: E is the embedding, t a transformer layer, m the MTP layer, L the loss (final norm and LM head), | ends a chunk, and *N repeats. Et*4|(t*4|)*14tmL is sixteen chunks: the embedding plus four layers, fourteen chunks of four layers, and a last chunk of one layer plus the MTP layer plus the loss. Note that the MTP layer counts as a chunk member: Megatron trains it, and it lands on the last rank.We continue not to model the MTP module, and here that is a bigger omission than on Hopper. On the last rank it adds a full MoE transformer layer (parameters, gradients, optimizer shard and activations like any other), a 103M-parameter projection, and a second copy of the 926M-parameter embedding table. The peak rank in this post is rank 0, which holds none of it. Click "fold" to see the chunks land on the two ranks:

Rank 0 gets the embedding and 32 layers; rank 1 gets 29 layers, the MTP layer and the head. That is the easy half. The interesting half is, again, how many microbatches' activations each rank holds. Interleaved 1F1B issues forwards chunk-major: rank 0 runs chunk 0 for PP microbatches, then its next chunk for the same PP microbatches, and so on down its VP chunks before the first backward arrives. Megatron's warmup on rank r is 2(PP − r − 1) + (VP − 1)·PP chunk-forwards, plus one more when the 1F1B all-to-all overlap is on (it is, in every MXFP8 recipe here), and the steady state issues one forward before each backward frees one. So at the peak rank r holds PP·VP + PP − 2r chunk-stashes: 18 on rank 0, 16 on rank 1. Select a rank below to see the lifetimes:

Divide by VP and that is 2.25 microbatches on rank 0 against DualPipeV's uniform PP + ½; averaged over the ranks it is exactly PP. Interleaving does not add activation memory on average, it flattens plain 1F1B's PP − r staircase toward uniform, adding (PP − 1)/VP on rank 0 and taking it from the last rank. (Its purpose is to shrink pipeline bubbles by a factor of VP; that is the timing post's story.) But the count hides where the stashes sit. The surplus above PP is not spread over a rank's chunks: it lands on the chunks the schedule visited first, so at rank 0's peak its first chunk holds four microbatches and the other seven hold two. The spreadsheet at the bottom charges each layer by the stashes held on its chunk at the moment the rank's stash bytes peak, which for a rank whose last chunk is light (rank 1's single layer plus MTP plus loss) is not the same moment as the count peak.

Activation checkpointing

Megatron exposes recompute as a list of modules, --recompute-granularity selective --recompute-modules …, rather than per-op marks. Each module name is a fixed set of marks in the diagram's terms, so the presets below are Megatron's names:

  • moe_act: drop the SwiGLU output and re-make it from the kept gate/up output — the DeepSeek paper's trick, and the one lever NVIDIA's GB300 BF16 recipe pulls.
  • mla_up_proj: drop the full q/k/v and re-make them from the kept latents (the fused up-projections re-run their latent norms too) — the GB200 recipes' lever.
  • layernorm: drop both norm outputs; core_attn: checkpoint the core attention; moe: checkpoint the entire MoE layer, router to combine (not allowed together with the all-to-all overlap).
  • mlp: checkpoint the dense MLP — in this model, the three dense layers and nothing else. The MLPerf GB200 submissions and the public guide use it; it is a rounding error on a 61-layer MoE model, so it is not a preset here.

The GB300 MXFP8 recipes recompute nothing. That is the first inversion of the Hopper post: on 80 GiB, recompute was mandatory; on 276 GiB it is a knob you reach for only in the BF16 variant.

Low precision: MXFP8

Blackwell's tensor cores scale 32-element blocks natively, with one power-of-two (E8M0) scale per block, so the recipe question that consumed half of the Hopper post, how to keep an activation usable in both GEMM orientations, has a different answer here. Transformer Engine quantizes a linear's input in both orientations at once, uses the row-wise copy for the forward GEMM, and keeps only the column-wise copy for the weight-gradient GEMM. One copy at 1 + 1/32 bytes per element: no transposed dual stash. The other stash facts of the recipe, as the diagram renders them:

  • Every GEMM runs MXFP8, attention included (fp8_dot_product_attention is on in the GB300 recipes, off in the GB200 one), and on GB300 so does the LM-head projection. We take FP8 attention to stash q, k and v in MXFP8 and its output in bf16.
  • The attention out-projection does not keep a quantized input at all: it is told to save its original input, which aliases the attention output the attention kernel already saved.
  • Megatron runs the q and kv down-projections as two separate linears, and each keeps its own quantized copy of the norm output, hence the ×2 on that chip. Megatron's fused SwiGLU saves its input in bf16.Two things we could not pin from source: what NVIDIA's fused grouped-MLP kernel (NVTE_CUTEDSL_FUSED_GROUPED_MLP) saves between fc1 and fc2, and what Transformer Engine's MXFP8 attention path saves. The diagram follows the unfused modules for both.
  • FP8 parameter gather keeps the compute parameters resident as MXFP8 tensors, but in both orientations, row- and column-wise, since neither block scaling can be derived from the other. That is 2 × (1 + 1/32) bytes per parameter: FP8 parameters save all-gather bytes, not memory. The bf16 gather itself lands in the gradient buffer (reuse_grad_buf_for_mxfp8_param_ag) and does not persist.

Not modeled, and worth knowing about before comparing this roofline to a real memory trace: NVIDIA's full-iteration CUDA graphs need static shapes, so the MoE buffers are padded to a fixed expert capacity (1.5× the balanced load in the archive recipe, 4× in the MLPerf submission, which routes for real) and the dispatched activations live in a paged stash with a CPU spill; the LM head's MXFP8 weight copies; and the MTP module described above.

Fitting it

The Hopper post stepped through the levers until the bar ducked under the line. On GB300 we run the same steps, and then keep going to see which levers were load-bearing.

Step 1 — Global memory usage. Everything replicated on every one of the 256 GPUs, with Megatron's bf16 gradient buffer.
Step 2 — ZeRO-1. Megatron's distributed optimizer shards the optimizer state (fp32 master weights, bf16 moments) over DP — here only 256 ways, not 2048.
Step 3 — Expert parallelism. EP32 divides the expert weights and gradients by 32. The expert optimizer shards divide only by the expert data parallelism, DP/EP = 8. Thirty-two GPUs is less than half an NVL72 rack, so the all-to-all never leaves the NVLink domain.
Step 4 — Pipeline parallelism, interleaved 1F1B. PP2 with VP8 halves the weights, gradients and optimizer states. Activations on rank 0 go from one microbatch in flight to 18 chunk-stashes, four of them on its first chunk.
Step 5 — MXFP8. The stashes shrink; the weights do not, because the resident MXFP8 parameters keep both orientations. This is NVIDIA's GB300 configuration: no recompute needed.
What if there were no pipeline at all? Drop PP entirely, keep EP32: the weights and gradients double back, the activations fall to one microbatch, and by this roofline it still fits on a GB300 with 40 GiB to spare. Memory is not why NVIDIA runs PP2 here.
The GB300 config on a GB200. The same configuration against 184 GiB. By this roofline it fits with 7 GiB to spare — and the intermediates the roofline leaves out (CUDA-graph pools, the 1.5× expert-capacity buffers, the paged stash, communicator workspaces) are certainly worth more than that. So NVIDIA's GB200 recipes move to PP4 / VP4 / EP64 with the MLA up-projections recomputed, and the MLPerf GB200 submissions add TP2 as well. On GB200, memory is still picking the configuration.

Conclusion

On Hopper every lever was load-bearing. On GB300 the reference configuration lands near 177 GiB of 276 with no recompute at all, and the roofline still fits with the pipeline removed. On GB200 the same configuration lands 7 GiB under the line, which is to say over it in practice, and NVIDIA's GB200 recipes duly pull more levers. So Blackwell splits the question the Hopper post could not: on GB200 memory still picks the configuration; on GB300 it does not, yet NVIDIA runs PP2 there anyway, and shards the optimizer rather than the parameters (ZeRO-1, not ZeRO-3). Those choices are not explained by anything in this post; they are communication rooflines, and they are what the next post is about.
