Study 00 — the DSv3 block diagram: what it shows and why

The one widget every other page leans on, on its own, with the reasoning behind it. See also study 01: a DSv3-family MoE on 2048 Hopper GPUs, study 02: the NVIDIA MLPerf 6.0 256×GB300 configuration, and the landing page for the sandbox with every widget linked together.

This diagram is not an illustration of a DeepSeek-V3 transformer block. It is the input surface for the memory and roofline models: the precision of every GEMM and the save-vs-recompute mark on every op live here, and the numbers on the other pages are computed from whatever state you leave it in. That one fact decides almost everything below — a picture you can only look at, and a picture you have to answer questions with, are different pictures.

Nothing is linked to this instance (no for= target), so it is safe to poke at. Its state persists in the URL hash, so a configuration you like is shareable; reset puts it back.

1. Structure — what is a box, and what isn't

A node is an op with a distinct backward appetite. The graph (src/blockgraph.js) exists to answer one question: if I refuse to stash this tensor, what do I have to replay, and what does that force me to stash instead? So the granularity rule is split whenever the answer can differ. A matmul's backward needs its input; a norm's backward needs its input plus the per-token rstd; flash attention needs its own output and the softmax lse; a residual add needs nothing at all. Those are four different behaviours, so they are four kinds of box.

Corollaries, in the order they bit us.

  • The q and kv up-projections are separate boxes. They were one box until we noticed they already carried separate precision markers — if two things can be marked differently, they cannot share a box.
  • The residual adds are ops. The ⊕ on the spine is the junction; the small dashed box beside it is the op, with its own 💾/↻ button. Earlier the tensor chip (x1) was the clickable thing, which made the residual the one control in the diagram that worked differently from every other control.
  • The a2a dispatch and combine are ops too (the violet boxes), because under full checkpointing the communication itself replays — a real and expensive consequence that a diagram which drew comms as mere arrows could not express.
  • The router is not just "probabilities". Production routers retain logits and scores (both fp32, even when the associated loss weights are zero), top-k weights and indices, and int32 routing maps — a little over 2 KiB/token, not the 1 KiB the probability vector suggests. The box is labelled "router state" for that reason. This one is implementation-specific and will need its own retention set per framework.
  • The combine's backward reads the SwiGLU output and the router state, not the expert outputs. Gate weights are applied to the swiglu output before the linear down-projection — mathematically identical to gating the expert outputs, and it makes the expert outputs a pure intermediate. That is a ~30 GiB difference at study-01 scale, and it is visible in the diagram only because the combine is allowed to declare non-obvious backward needs.

Marks go on ops; annotations on wires are derived. You choose a policy — save this op's output, or replay this op in backward — and the diagram computes the consequence: the transitive replay closure, and therefore which tensors are forced to stay stashed. Every wire label is an output, tagged with what happened to it: ↓ ↑ ⇅ saved and read by the op below / the producer's own backward / both, ↻ recomputed, · not needed, 🔒 always saved. The pedagogy is in the derivation: marking one op ↻ visibly turns some neighbouring · into ↓. A diagram that let you mark tensors directly would let you express incoherent policies and would teach nothing.

The block is the checkpoint region. Its input x0 is always saved (🔒) — it is the anchor every replay chain terminates at — and its output x2 is the next block's x0, charged there, not here. Fixing the boundary this way is what makes one block's inventory composable by multiplication instead of double-counting the residual stream. Aux artifacts (rstd, lse) exit their box to the right rather than down the spine, because they are not dataflow; they are saved unless their op replays, since a replay regenerates them for free.

What is deliberately not here. Parallelism (that's the controls strip), the watermark itself (that's the memory bars), forward temporaries (not set in stone, and not the point), the layer structure — the real family stacks 3 dense blocks before the sparse ones, which is a separate <dsv3-stack> widget so this one can stay about a single block. RoPE is fused into the q/kv paths and always recomputed (negligible), so it is footnoted rather than drawn. The shared expert and the dense MLP follow the ffn boxes' choices. The MLA-internal norms are the one asymmetry we have not resolved: the diagram draws the two block-level RMSNorms and not the norms inside MLA.

The head is drawn once, below a dashed rule. Everything above the rule is one of ×61 blocks; below it are the final norm, the lm head, and the loss. Keeping them on the page — rather than in a separate figure — is the point of the combined view: at ×61 the head stops looking like the big thing, which is the intuition most people get wrong.

2. The two block grids — quantities, in situ

Both encodings answer "how big, relative to the other choices I could make", so both are counted in little squares rather than printed as numbers you would have to compare arithmetically.

  • Orange grid, under a saved tensor: bytes. ▪ = 4 KB/token. In the combined view the chip's number is the real total — ×layers × in-flight microbatches × tokens — so it tallies with the memory bars on the study pages when you set those two display knobs to your PP stage.
  • Coloured strip, inside an op box: FLOPs as time at peak. Scaled so the largest op in the block fills exactly one 30-square row, with mxfp8 counted half (2× peak) and fp32 double (half peak), so the strips read as duration rather than arithmetic. The lm head uses the same scale, divided by the layer count in combined view — per-token vocab work, independent of depth. Norms and SwiGLU get a muted fig-leaf square: they are bandwidth-bound and their compute precision is unspecified, and pretending otherwise would be a lie told in colour.
  • The tally at the right of the caption totals fwd (1×) + bwd (2×, dgrad + wgrad) + replay, in the same squares at the same scale. It exists so the cost of recompute is not an abstraction: mark ops ↻ and the replay row grows under your cursor.

Colour means dtype, everywhere: dark bf16, blue mxfp8, plum fp32 — on FLOP strips and on saved-tensor tags alike, and sorted into contiguous runs within a tally row so each colour is one block. The stash tag also states the dtype the simulator ascribes to that tensor (the dtype of the matmul whose backward reads it), because that is a genuine degree of freedom and hiding it would hide an assumption.

3. Aesthetics — verticality and the rest

Dataflow runs top-down; the spine runs down the left of each column. The vertical axis is natural for a residual stream, and the wire is shunted to the left edge rather than centred because the right of the spine is where the payload goes: every output gets a tensor chip and possibly a grid of squares. Boxes were also flattened (they had a lot of whitespace) to buy that room back.

Two columns, MLA and MoE, not one tall one. A single column was correct and unusable: you could not see the block and its outputs — the memory bars, the trace — on one screen, and the whole point is to watch the outputs move while you toggle. Splitting the block at the first residual add halves the height. For the same reason the FLOP tally floats to the right of the caption (there was blank space there anyway), and on the study pages the whole widget is narrow enough to sit beside its outputs.

Residual wires take the long way around, once. The first residual runs down a rail on the far left and comes in at the ⊕. The second does not swing up and back down again; the ⊕ has a single output downward, and the feed to the second RMSNorm branches off the bottom rail at a junction dot. Junction dots are kept clear of arrowheads, and the x1 chip sits clear of the wire. These are small, but a diagram that people are supposed to read as a graph has to be unambiguous about where the wires actually go.

Nothing reflows. Chip space is reserved for the worst case (saved, bf16) whether or not the tensor is currently saved, so toggling a mark or a precision changes colours and glyphs but never moves a box. Layout that jumps under an interaction destroys the comparison you were making.

Two colours with two jobs, no overlap. Orange means saved for backward — a state. Violet means communication — a kind. They were briefly conflated (dashed orange for the a2a boxes, which read as "recompute", which it is not), and separating them is the difference between a legend you can hold in your head and one you have to keep re-reading. Dashed outlines are reserved for the residual-add boxes, which are ops in the model but not really boxes in the picture.

Nothing is encoded by colour alone. Every state has a glyph: ↓ ↑ ⇅ ↻ · 🔒 ←, and the mode button reads 💾 or ↻ in text. The dtype buttons print the dtype name.

Controls are uniform and adjacent. Every markable op has the same 24 px 💾/↻ button in the same corner, with the dtype button next to it, same size, everywhere. One click cycles precision (bf16 → mxfp8 → fp32); one click flips the mark. Presets recognise themselves: toggle away from a preset and the selector says custom, toggle back and it says the preset's name again.

Tooltips are instant, light, pinnable, and rationed. They appear on hover with no delay, click to pin (orange border) and click again to dismiss; the card is light, not a black bubble. They are attached only to op boxes and the small grey dimension strings — the two places where a number needs explaining — and the hit target is the whole box, not just its label. Each op's tooltip gives the per-token FLOP count and the expression it came from, so the model can be checked rather than trusted.

4. How it plugs in

Set for="id1 id2" to the ids of a <dsv3-memory> or <dsv3-trace>, and every edit here patches those widgets' precision and save-marks; recipe and recompute pick the starting preset, and xlayers / xinflight the combined-view multipliers. A <dsv3-controls> strip can drive the schematic and the outputs together through hardware/parallelism bundles. All interactive state round-trips through the URL hash.
